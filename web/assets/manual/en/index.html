Cantus — quick-start manual

1. Install

Cantus runs on Windows, macOS and Linux; every release has one file for each, on the releases page and behind the download button of cantusdaw.com, which gives you your system's:

  • Windows 11, x64: Cantus-<version>-windows-x64-setup.exe (or the portable zip).
  • macOS, Apple silicon or Intel: Cantus-<version>-macos-universal.dmg.
  • Linux x86-64, Ubuntu 22.04 or newer (or alike): Cantus-<version>-linux-x86_64.AppImage.

SHA256SUMS.txt beside them lets you check a download: Get-FileHash <file> on Windows, shasum -a 256 <file> on a Mac, sha256sum <file> on Linux. About 200 MB of disk. Setting up your audio on each system is chapter 2.

On Windows

You need Windows 11, 64-bit (Windows 10 untested).

  1. Download Cantus-0.1.0-beta.1-windows-x64-setup.exe from the releases page.
  2. Run it. It installs for you alone, in %LOCALAPPDATA%\Programs\Cantus, and asks for no administrator rights. This beta is not code-signed yet, so Windows may show "Windows protected your PC": choose More info ▸ Run anyway.
  3. Choose the components: the program (required), Open .cantus projects with Cantus (on), a desktop shortcut (off), the CLAP and VST3 plug-ins for other DAWs (on), and FlexASIO driver (downloaded now) (off — tick it if your sound card has no ASIO driver of its own; see chapter 2).
  4. The Privacy page says what Cantus sends over the network (only a daily question about new versions, and the downloads you ask for). Check for updates once a day (recommended) is ticked; untick it to turn the check off (also later in Settings ▸ Updates).

Portable copy. Unzip Cantus-0.1.0-beta.1-windows-x64-portable.zip anywhere (a USB stick works). Because of the portable.txt beside cantus.exe, that copy keeps its settings in the data folder next to it and writes nothing to your profile.

Uninstall from Windows' Apps. Your settings, presets and autosaves — and the sample cache (up to 16 GB, see chapter 11) — are removed only if you tick that box; your projects are never touched.

On a Mac

  1. Download Cantus-<version>-macos-universal.dmg — one disk image for Apple silicon and Intel Macs.
  2. Open it and drag Cantus onto Applications. Eject the disk image.
  3. Open Cantus from Applications (or Launchpad). A notarised release opens straight away; one that is not yet notarised makes macOS say it cannot verify the developer: right-click Cantus ▸ Open ▸ Open once (or System Settings ▸ Privacy & Security ▸ Open Anyway), and it opens normally from then on.
  4. The first time you record, macOS asks whether Cantus may use the microphone: allow it (later in System Settings ▸ Privacy & Security ▸ Microphone).

Cantus keeps its settings, presets and autosaves in ~/Library/Application Support/Cantus. Uninstall by moving Cantus from Applications to the Bin; that folder is yours to delete or keep.

On Linux

  1. Download Cantus-<version>-linux-x86_64.AppImage — one file that runs on most distributions from the last few years, with nothing to install and no administrator.
  2. Make it executable and open it: in a file manager, Properties ▸ Permissions ▸ Allow executing as a program, or in a terminal chmod +x Cantus-*.AppImage && ./Cantus-*.AppImage. Keep it where you like (a folder such as ~/Applications is a good home): Cantus updates that file in place.
  3. If it does not start and says FUSE is missing, install your distribution's libfuse2 (Ubuntu 22.04: sudo apt install libfuse2; 24.04: libfuse2t64).

Cantus keeps its settings in ~/.config/cantus and its presets, autosaves and sounds in ~/.local/share/cantus (or wherever $XDG_CONFIG_HOME and $XDG_DATA_HOME say). Uninstall by deleting the AppImage. A Cantus from your distribution's packages or from Flatpak is installed and updated by them instead.

2. First run: set up your audio

The first time Cantus starts, the window Set up your audio opens over the music.

  1. Choose your interface. Each audio device has a card: an Audio interface, This computer, Headphones… Click the one you play and listen through. Cantus picks a driver and a buffer for it: ASIO at 128 samples for an interface with its own ASIO driver, otherwise WASAPI (Windows' own audio) at 256.
  2. Listen. Click Test tone: you should hear a soft tone on outputs 1–2.
  3. Play or speak — the meter should move. Strum, sing or speak into what is plugged in; the input that moves is the one you will record from.
  4. Latency. Cantus says how long the sound takes from string to speaker, and whether that is tight enough to play through. For the most accurate takes, cable output 1 to input 1 and click Measure latency; Cantus then places every take to the sample.

Click Record on … and Cantus adds an audio track on that input, armed and ready: press R to record. Skip closes the window; it does not open by itself again (the Setup assistant… button in the audio chip brings it back).

On a Mac the driver is Core Audio, which every interface uses: there is no ASIO to install. Choose the buffer (128 samples is a good start) and the rate on the device page; an aggregate device made in Audio MIDI Setup (two interfaces as one) is listed like any other. The first time you record, macOS asks whether Cantus may use the microphone — allow it, or no input reaches Cantus (System Settings ▸ Privacy & Security ▸ Microphone). Cantus's audio threads work with Core Audio's own as one group, so Apple silicon runs them on its fast cores.

The audio chip

The chip in the top bar (or in its ⋯ menu when the window is narrow) shows the device, driver, rate, buffer and latency — for example UMC204HD · ASIO · 48 k · 128 · 6.1 ms. Its lamp is green when the audio runs, amber after a dropout, red when the device is lost or the driver stopped. Click it to change anything; every change applies at once and is remembered for that interface:

  • Interface, Driver (WASAPI or ASIO, with the ASIO driver list), Input device, Rate;
  • Buffer · round trip: 64 to 2048 samples, each with its latency. Smaller is tighter for playing; larger is safer for big mixes. If the top bar counts dropouts, take the next size up;
  • problems appear on top with a button that fixes them (Restart audio, Use this computer's audio, Run at 48 kHz…);
  • Test tone, Channels & routing… (name your inputs and outputs, choose a headphone cue pair), Setup assistant…, and the gear for every setting.

Which driver? Use your interface's own ASIO driver if it has one (Behringer, Focusrite, PreSonus, Steinberg, MOTU, RME…). For a laptop's built-in sound, use WASAPI, or install FlexASIO for lower latency. Get ASIO… in the popover opens a window with FlexASIO's download page, ASIO4ALL's page and the makers' driver pages; after installing a driver, click Rescan drivers. Cantus never installs drivers by itself.

Plugging an interface in offers to switch to it; unplugging one offers your computer's audio, and Cantus goes back to it when it returns. If a driver stops responding, Cantus restarts the audio once by itself. You cannot change the device while recording.

3. The workspace

Cantus opens on a song — the second drop of the demo Neon Tide, looping — so the first thing you hear is music. Press Space to play it.

  • The top bar (see below): the menus over the project's name and the hint panel, then the transport, the tempo, the metronome, the position, the meters and more — one group per function.
  • The arrangement (centre): tracks and their clips on a timeline.
  • The clip browser (Alt+F5, View ▸ Clip browser): every clip of the song as a tile, beside the arrangement (see chapter 5).
  • The lower zone: the Mixer, the Piano roll and the Channel rack as tabs.
  • The inspector (right, I): everything about the selected track, strip or clip.
  • The status bar (bottom): what is under the pointer, plug-in latency, samples loading, the typing keyboard's octave and minimised devices.

Every area can be shown or hidden with its window key or its function key (F5 arrangement, Alt+F5 clip browser, F6 channel rack, F7 piano roll, F9 mixer, Alt+F8 browser, I inspector). Drag the splitters to resize; right-click a tab to float it in its own window; save the arrangement of the window under View ▸ Layout ▸ Save layout…. The interface zooms from 75 % to 200 % (Ctrl+Plus, Ctrl+Minus, Ctrl+0). Twelve themes — Cantus Night (the default), Cantus Paper, Daylight, Blue Hour, high-contrast dark and light and more, each with a preview — the colours, the logo and the taskbar icon's colour are under View ▸ Appearance.

Typing a name or a value works the same everywhere — renaming a track, a clip, a pad or a strip, a search, a level: the field opens with its words selected, so typing replaces them; Enter or a click elsewhere keeps what you typed; Esc puts back what was there. While you type, no shortcut fires. Names can be in any script — Japanese, Chinese, Korean, Hindi, Thai, Arabic, Hebrew…: Cantus uses your system's own fonts for them (see the known issues about right-to-left scripts).

Demo songs. File ▸ Demo songs… (Ctrl+Shift+N) opens seven songs made entirely with Cantus's own instruments and effects: With All Your Heart (cinematic pop — the modelled grand Chorda pedalled from its automation lane, every modulation and dynamics effect of round 28, Tonus hard-tuning the topline, modulators and automation, mastered to −11 LUFS), Neon Tide (house), Morning Light (pop ballad), Sunday Strut (funk and soul), Paper Lanterns (lo-fi hip-hop), Aurora (cinematic) and Midnight Static (trap). Open one, play it, take it apart.

The command palette

Ctrl+Shift+P (View ▸ Command palette…) opens a field near the top of the window that finds anything Cantus can do. Type a few letters — of any word, in your language or in English: mix finds Show the mixer, new tr New track with…, spl Split clip — and the list narrows as you type; the arrows move, Enter runs the row, Esc closes. It holds:

  • every action with its shortcut at the right (the same as the menus and the keys);
  • the areas and windows (the devices panel, the shortcuts, the appearance editor, the start page…) and every Settings page (Settings: Audio…);
  • every effect — inserted on the selected strip (or the selected track's, or the master) — and every instrument, on a new track after the selected one; your own track templates too;
  • the recent projects and the project templates (see Templates).

What you choose often and lately comes first; with nothing typed, the list starts with what you used last.

The undo history

Alt+H (View ▸ Show the undo history) shows every step of the project's history, beside the inspector — dock it, stack it or float it like any area. Each step is named by what it did — Move 3 notes, Insert Concentus on Lead, Rename track to Bass — under a first row for the project as it was opened. The current step is lit; the steps after it, the ones Redo would bring back, are dimmed; the step your file holds says SAVED, and the steps since the last save carry a dot. Click any row to go there: Cantus undoes (or redoes) each step between, as Ctrl+Z would — nothing is lost, and a click on a later row comes back.

The top bar

The top bar is FL Studio's strip in two rows: one group per function, a thin divider between the columns. The groups with two lines of their own — PAT / SONG, the metronome, the clock, CPU/RAM and the meter — take the bar's height; the others are one row high and stack two to a column (the transport over the tempo, the snap over undo, the windows over the inputs, the audio chip over the tools). At 1920 pixels and 100 % every group is on the bar, in English and in Spanish, with your interface's chip on it: on a full bar the chip shortens a long device line in the middle (point at it for the whole line), and widens when there is room.

  • The menus and the hint panel: File, Edit, Clip, View, Help over the project's name and the hint for whatever is under the pointer. Click the name to rename the project in place (Enter keeps it, Esc leaves); the window's title follows it, with • while there are unsaved changes.
  • PAT / SONG (L): song mode plays the arrangement; pattern mode plays the clip you are editing in the piano roll or the channel rack — with whatever the rack shows on the other tracks in that stretch — alone and looped, and the clock counts from the pattern's start; the playhead stays in the pattern (Home or a click outside it goes to its top). Choosing another note clip changes what plays; selecting a track, an audio clip or nothing keeps the pattern. Leaving pattern mode puts the playhead back where the song was. Pattern mode does not record, and cannot be switched on in the middle of a take (a take belongs to the song: switch to song mode first) and export always renders the song. It is saved with the project.
  • Transport: go to start, play, stop, record and its ▾ options, loop.
  • Tempo: the BPM (drag, wheel, double-click for 120); TAP (Shift+T): tap at least twice — the tempo is the average of your last taps, a pause starts again, and each run of taps is one undo step; the time signature: click it for the beats (‹ ›), the note value and the usual metres.
  • Metronome (Ctrl+M): its switch, the count-in (off, 1, 2, 4 bars) and the click's level (drag, wheel, double-click for 0 dB). Its ▾ holds the sound (Classic, Woodblock, Beep), the accent on the first beat of each bar, and when it plays (always, while recording, during the count-in). The sound and the accent are saved with the song.
  • Position: bars.beats.ticks and the time, the metre beside them; while recording, the take's time.
  • Grid and snap: the grid and the SNAP lamp of the editor you are working in — the arrangement, the piano roll or the channel rack (always on its grid); with an audio clip in the piano roll's place, the pitch editor's grid (what Quantise timing moves notes to), and with an automation clip, the arrangement's grid the automation editor snaps to. The same values as the editor's own toolbar.
  • Undo and redo, and the window keys (browser, arrangement, rack, piano roll, mixer, inspector).
  • CPU, RAM, voices, drops: the audio callback's load (its histogram in the tooltip), the memory Cantus uses, the voices sounding and the dropouts counted (the latest one's cause in the tooltip, and said on the status line when it happens).
  • Master meter: Peak shows the left and right peaks and holds the highest of the last two seconds (click the number to reset it); LUFS shows the master's loudness — momentary in the top lane, short-term below, with a mark at −14 LUFS (the streaming services' level), and underneath the short-term, the integrated loudness of the pass (from Play; it holds after Stop) and the highest true peak since you pressed play (click the number to reset it).
  • Inputs: the typing keyboard (Ctrl+T), the MIDI lamp (it lights as notes come in; the number is the inputs open) and Armed (how many tracks will record; click to arm the selected one).
  • Sync (shown while Link or an outside clock is followed, or when you show it in Customise top bar…): LINK and its peers, Q (Link's quantum) and EXT (the source's tempo and lock light) — see Playing in sync.
  • The audio chip (see the audio chip) and the tools: the element picker, the appearance page, and the interface's zoom.

A narrow window folds the least important groups — whole — into the ⋯ menu at the right end, where they work as on the bar — their own menus and popovers included; a group folded earlier comes back as soon as it fits. The menus never fold: they sit above the hint panel, in its width. A dot on ⋯ says something is folded there (red when it is the audio chip and the audio needs a look). Customise top bar… (in ⋯, in View, or right-click the bar): tick a group to show or hide it, drag it by its grip to move it, Reset for the default. Your arrangement is kept.

4. Record audio and MIDI

An audio track

  1. Add an audio track: Ctrl+Shift+A, or right-click the + above the track headers ▸ Add audio track. (The first-run setup's Record on … makes one for you.)
  2. Arm it: click the round red lamp on its header (or Alt+R). Shift+click arms that track alone. An armed track shows its input meter.
  3. Choose its input: the input chip on the header (In 1, In 1-2…; shown when the track row is tall enough), the inspector's Recording section, or the track's menu.
  4. Monitoring: the speaker key on the header cycles Off, Auto (you hear the input while the track is armed — the default) and On. The inspector also has Trim (−24 to +24 dB before recording).
  5. Press R (or the record key). Every armed track records; press R or Space to stop. The whole pass is one undo step.

Takes are written as 32-bit float WAV files: into the project's Audio folder once it is saved, until then into %LOCALAPPDATA%\Cantus\recordings\; saving moves them into the project. Each take is placed earlier by the measured latency, so it lines up with what you heard.

Recording options

The ▾ beside the record key (or right-click it) opens Recording options:

  • Count-in: off, 1, 2 or 4 bars before the take (Alt+C). The count-in clicks whether or not the Metronome (Ctrl+M) is on. It plays before recording from stop — the top bar's chip says so with a small red dot, and its menu under Before recording — as in FL Studio and Ableton; Play alone starts at once. To hear it before playing too, tick Also on play in the chip's menu (or the metronome's options ▾): off by default, kept for every project.
  • Punch: record only inside a range. From selection takes the time selection; Alt+P uses the selection, else the loop, else two bars from the playhead. The range shows as a red brace under the ruler: drag its ends. Everything played is kept in the file; punch only sets the clip's window.
  • Loop takes: with the loop on, each pass becomes a take.
  • Take mode: Stack (old clips move to take lanes — the default), Replace (cut what the take covers) or Overdub (MIDI: add notes to the clip).
  • The click — its level, sound, accent and when it plays — is the metronome's, in the top bar.

Monitoring with low latency

Delay compensation keeps every track in time with the slowest one: with a linear-phase EQ somewhere in the song, everything — the input you monitor included — waits for it, and a singer can hear themself 96 ms late. Low-latency monitoring (Alt+Shift+R, or its switch in the record options, in the audio chip's popover and in the inspector's recording rows) changes that while it is on:

  • What you monitor (an armed track's input in Auto, any input set to On, or an armed instrument track you play) reaches your ears without waiting for the other tracks: a voice through Tonus is heard 2.7 ms late instead of 96 ms. The backing stays in time with itself.
  • An insert on the monitored path whose latency is above the limit (3 ms by default; 5, 10 or 20 ms in the same rows) is bypassed — the list says which: Monitoring without: Aequalis, Culmen (and how many more, past eight). They come back the moment you switch it off, with nothing left over from before.
  • The audio chip shows the round trip of what you monitor — the driver's, the input's wait (for an audio input: notes you play only go out) and the monitored chain's — while it is on.
  • Your take is placed exactly where it would be with it off: what is recorded is the raw input, placed by the latency in force when the take started. If the backing's latency changes during the take (you switch the mode, an automated look-ahead), what you play after is placed by the new one: every part lands where you played it.

For the lowest delay, monitor through a strip of your own: a bus that mixes your voice with other recorded tracks keeps those in time, and your voice waits there with them.

Takes and comping

Every take is kept on a take lane under the track: open them with the small disclosure at the bottom of the track's colour cell, Alt+T, or the track menu's Show takes. Drag across a take to comp that stretch into the track; click a take to use all of it. Right-click a lane for Use this take, Delete unused takes (which also drops recordings nothing plays any more), Explode takes to new tracks and Hide takes.

Seams. Where two takes meet in the comp they crossfade: equal-power, 10 ms by default, centred on the seam, each take reading on into its own recording. A seam has a small diamond at the foot of the track; drag it (or the edge of either take there) and both sides move together. Right-click it for its crossfade — Off, 5, 10, 20 or 50 ms — or to use that one for every seam of the track. Each piece of the comp shows the number of its take, and the take lanes light what is in the comp.

Alternate comps. The first take lane's header has the comp's chip, Comp A. Click it for the comps: switch between them (each comes back exactly as you left it, pitch edits included), New comp (a copy of this one, to try another idea), Rename, Delete, Flatten (keep the comp, drop the takes) and Flatten and merge (the comp bounced into one recording beside the takes — their files stay on disk). The same menu turns Quick swipe off (then dragging on a lane selects time and a click selects a take) and grouped comping on or off: tracks whose strips are in one mixer group — a drum kit's microphones recorded together — comp together, the same stretch of the same take on each; their seams move together, take the same crossfades, and their comps switch and are made together. Every change is one undo step.

What belongs to the comp. The pieces a swipe or a promote makes are the comp's, and so is the take that plays over the others after a recording (the last pass of a loop, or a take recorded over older ones; a recording where no take lies is a plain clip that plays under every comp): they switch with the comp, and stay its own when split, nudged or slipped. A copy is never the comp's, wherever it is pasted — right over its take too: it stays on the playing lane through every switch, under every comp. A piece dragged to another track leaves the comp; after a flatten the pieces are plain clips. The take lanes light the stretch of each take the comp plays.

MIDI

Arm an instrument track (one with no audio input) and press R: notes from every MIDI keyboard Cantus found (the top bar's MIDI lamp lights as notes come in; its tooltip names the inputs) are recorded into a clip. With nothing armed, R records into the selected track. No keyboard? Ctrl+T turns the typing keyboard on: A–L play notes, Z/X change octave. While it is on, letter shortcuts (including R) are off — use Space or the record key. The keyboard's pitch bend, mod wheel, pedals, every other controller (CC 0–119), channel pressure and how fast you let each key go (release velocity) are recorded too: they land in the clip's lanes under the notes in the piano roll.

MPE: per-note expression

An MPE controller (a Seaboard, a LinnStrument, an Osmose, a Sensel) gives each note its own pitch bend, pressure and timbre (slide, CC 74). Cantus records them on the notes, shows and edits them in the piano roll, and plays them to its instruments and to plug-ins.

  • Turn it on. Settings ▸ MIDI ▸ MPE: set the controller's port to MPE. Other ports are untouched — a keyboard on channel 5 is never read as an MPE member. Zones: a lower zone of 15 member channels is what most controllers send (members ±48 semitones, master ±2); choose Upper or Both for a split controller. A controller that sends its MCM sets its own zones and bend ranges.
  • Playing and recording. Each note is heard with its own bend, pressure and timbre, live and while recording; a take keeps them on its notes (thinned to what an ear tells apart). The master channel's bend, pedal and wheel stay the track's lanes, as on any keyboard.
  • In the piano roll. A note's bend is the line through it (a semitone reaches the next row), its pressure the line's weight, its timbre its colour. The lane area's + menu shows a row per kind — Bend, Pressure, Timbre (per note) — with the selected notes' curves (every note's when none is selected): draw with the pencil or the line (Shift snaps a bend to whole semitones), right-click to smooth, thin, scale, flatten or clear. Each gesture is one undo step. Slicing, moving, transposing, quantising, copying and linked copies keep each note's curves with it.
  • Our instruments. Unda, Calor, Modulus and Gravis bend each note by its curve (smoothly, glide-safe). A fresh Unda, Calor or Modulus comes with pressure and timbre already routed in its last two matrix slots (Unda: level and wavetable position; Calor: its filter; Modulus: level and brightness) — ordinary routes you can change or remove.
  • Plug-ins. The track's inspector ▸ Note expression: Automatic (what the plug-in says it takes), Note expressions (CLAP note expressions, VST3 note expression), MPE (MIDI) (each note on a member channel of its own, with the MCM and a ±48 bend range — for plug-ins that take MIDI only) or Off.
  • Files. MIDI files: an MPE track is written as a lower zone (its MCM, the bend range, a member channel per note); an MPE file — with its MCM or recognised by how it plays — is read back as one track with each note's curves; key (poly) pressure is kept. DAWproject carries the curves as the notes' own expressions.

MIDI controllers

Any knob, fader, button or pad of a MIDI controller can drive anything Cantus can set: a parameter of any device (Cantus's own or a hosted plug-in's), a fader, a pan, a width, a send, a modulator's amount, a mute, a solo, a record arm, an insert's bypass or mix, the tempo, and the transport (play, stop, record, loop, the metronome, tap tempo, the markers, PAT / SONG — anything a key can do).

Learn in two clicks. Right-click a control ▸ Learn MIDI controller, then move a control on your hardware: done. Or click the top bar's MIDI cell (or press Ctrl+Shift+M; a right-click on it opens the MIDI settings) for the learn mode: every control that can be learnt is outlined; click one, move a hardware control, click the next. A mapped control wears a small dot at its lower right; in the learn mode it is outlined in the highlight's colour, and the one waiting for your hardware is lit. Esc lets go of a learn (so do 15 seconds without a move). With no MIDI input open, a click on the cell opens the MIDI settings instead. Change MIDI controller and Forget MIDI controller are in the same menu. The mixer's strip and insert menus and the track header's menu have Learn rows for the volume, pan, width, mute, solo, arm, bypass and mix, and a strip's for its effects switch and record arm (the strip's FX bulb and record dot learn in the learn mode too); a send's menu has them for its level; the channel rack's knobs and lamps, and a device's switches and choices, learn like any knob. In the inspector an insert's lamp (bypass) and ring (mix), the inserts' switch and the arm LED learn too, and so does a device window's bypass lamp. A control that stands for one value of a choice learns that value: a band's Solo LED learns that band (a button then solos it and lets it go), a key of Tonus's scale learns its click. Inside a plug-in's own window, move the parameter there, then press Alt+Shift+A (Link the last tweaked parameter to a controller, FL Studio's way — also in the top bar's Clip menu) and move the hardware.

Cantus tells what you moved: a plain knob, a 14-bit fader (its fine controller came too), an endless encoder (and which of the three encodings it speaks), a button, a pad (on a switch it toggles; on a knob its velocity sets the value) or a program change (the bank select a keyboard sends before it is passed over). Press a button and let it go: pressed and released it toggles; a button that stays down by itself (it sends its top value only) sets the target on and off as it is. Any parameter with two states (a device's on/off, a lamp) is a switch: a button sets it at once, with no takeover. NRPN and RPN controls are not learnt — Cantus says so and keeps waiting for another control; set the controller to send plain CCs.

How a mapping reads its control — in the Controllers pane (View menu, or Ctrl+Shift+K), one row per mapping:

  • Mode: Absolute (where the control is, the target is), Relative (an encoder: each step moves the target — on a choice, one choice per step), Toggle (each press flips it), Momentary (held: on; let go: off), Trigger (each press fires an action).
  • From / To: the part of the target's travel the control covers, in the target's own units (−∞ dB to +6 dB, 200 Hz to 4 kHz, 60 to 180 BPM…), a choice by its names (Clean to Opto, Linear phase to Minimum phase; a plug-in's choices by the plug-in's own words), a switch with no names Off and On; To below From turns it round. Drag a cell, or double-click it and type — a number, or a choice's name; right-click resets it.
  • Curve: a bend, so the first half of the turn does less (or more); typed in percent (−100 to 100), kept as typed.
  • Takeover, for a control that does not move itself when the value changes elsewhere: Pick up (the default: nothing moves until the knob passes the value — no jumps), Scale (the value moves with the knob, so both reach the end together), Jump. The header sets the takeover new mappings get; a switch has none.
  • Feedback (Send): the value goes back to the controller whenever it changes — for motor faders and LED rings, and the lights of the transport's buttons and every switch with a state (play, record, loop, the metronome, punch, count-in…). Off by default; an action that has no state (stop, undo) has no Send. What a controller or a loopback port sends straight back is recognised as the echo it is.

A lit dot at the left of a row shows a message just came. Shared marks one control on several targets (allowed: one knob can open two filters at once). A row whose target is gone (a deleted track) is kept, marked Missing, and works again if undo brings the target back. A plug-in's parameter is learnt with that plug-in: after the instrument or the insert is swapped for another — a plug-in, or one of Cantus's own — the row says Other plug-in, still named after the plug-in and the parameter it was learnt on, its range in percent, and its knob is free (learn the new one's parameter; an undo of the swap brings the old row back to life). An insert swapped for one of Cantus's own keeps its bypass and mix, and a parameter the new effect also has. Not loaded says the plug-in is in the project but not running here (or it stopped); Frozen, that the target's sound is frozen — a move is refused and said, and the knob reaches no other instrument; Not connected, that the mapping's MIDI input is not open; No input, that a strip's record arm has no track with an input to arm.

Two maps. This project's mappings are saved with the project, and every change is one undo step. Everywhere — your global mappings, kept beside your keyboard shortcuts — holds what means the same in every song: the transport, the tempo, the master, the selected track and the selected channel (volume, pan, mute, solo, arm — the master's when the master is selected) and the focused device's first eight knobs (the device window you clicked last). Learning a transport key or the master puts it there; Add a mapping in the pane offers all of them, and every keyboard shortcut's action by its group (arm the selected track, punch, undo, save, the views…). When one control is in both, the project's mapping wins. If the global file cannot be read whole, a copy is kept beside it (the status says its name) and nothing is lost; if not even a copy can be made, it is never written over, and each change to the global mappings says it will not be kept.

While you play and record. A mapped knob no longer reaches the instrument (it does not also bend its filter), and a mapped pad plays no note. A move made with the mouse or a controller is one undo step per gesture. With a parameter under an automation clip, the clip plays it — until a recording pass moves it: from that moment the parameter follows your hand (the clip is set aside for the rest of the pass), and the pass writes what you did as an automation clip, each move at the moment its message arrived, inside the pass (the count-in and anything past the punch-out are not written, and past the punch-out the parameter follows its clip again; Record options ▸ Record automation). While audio records, a knob mapped to the tempo does nothing (the status says why): change the tempo before or after the take.

Channel pressure and key pressure now reach the instrument (Cantus's and hosted ones), and a program change reaches a hosted instrument.

Playing in sync: Ableton Link, MIDI clock and time code

Cantus follows one outside clock at a time, and sends its own clock to the ports you choose. The top bar's Sync group shows it: LINK (its lamp lit while Link is on, the number of other apps in the session beside it), Q (Link's quantum, in beats) and EXT (the source followed: its lamp is the lock light — lit when locked, amber while pulling in or waiting for its moment — and the tempo it plays). Customise top bar… shows or hides the group like any other.

Ableton Link. Click LINK: Cantus joins the Link session of every app on your network (Live, Bitwig, Traktor, phone apps, another Cantus). The session's tempo becomes Cantus's while Link is on — the project's own tempo and its tempo track are kept and come back when Link is off (as in Live, Link overrides them while it is on). The top bar's tempo shows the session's; dragging it changes the session's tempo for everyone, and so does a change of the project's tempo. Press play and Cantus starts on the next quantum (Q: a click steps 1, 2, 3, 4, 8 beats; 4 is a bar of 4/4), in phase with the others; nothing already played moves, and the sound leaves the speakers when the session's beat does (the audio device's latency is allowed for). With Start/stop sync on (Settings ▸ MIDI), a play or a stop in any app starts or stops all of them. The first time Link is turned on, Windows may ask to let Cantus through its firewall (Link talks to the other apps over UDP on your network, as Live does): allow it on private networks. If Link cannot open the network, its lamp turns red and its tooltip says so.

MIDI clock in. In Settings ▸ MIDI, tick Clock on the input the master device is on (or choose Follow ▸ Clock and the port). Cantus then waits for the master: its Start plays from the beginning, Continue from its song position, Stop stops; the tempo follows the master's clocks, filtered so a jittery clock still plays steadily. Your own play key waits for the master too.

MIDI time code in. Tick MTC on the input instead: Cantus chases the code — a full frame locates, quarter frames run it, a short dropout is ridden through, and it stops when the code stops. Start code says which time code is the song's start (an hour, 01:00:00:00, is common); the project's tempo map turns the time into bars.

Clock and time code out. In the Outputs table, tick Clock on a port to send MIDI clock (24 per beat, the song position pointer, start, stop and continue, following the tempo track) and MTC to send time code at the Code rate chosen (24, 25, 29.97 drop-frame or 30) from the Start code. Each output has an Offset (‹ › in half milliseconds, a click sets zero) for a device that answers late; Feedback says whether controller lights and motor faders may use the port.

The ports' table. Each input's Notes and Controls can be turned off (a keyboard that should only send clock, a controller that should only drive the map); keys and pedals already down are still let go. On an MPE port, Controls carry each note's bend, pressure and timbre, so turned off they go silent.

  • MPE: the port's MPE zones and bend ranges (see MPE in the piano roll's pages).
  • Grid: the controller grid (Launchpad, APC) the port is, mirroring the session view.

The session view while following. A clip or scene launched from stop waits for the source: with Link, the next quantum (or the session's start, with Start/stop sync); with MIDI clock or time code, the master's Start. A scene's own tempo is proposed to the Link session at its launch, so the other apps change with it.

5. Edit: clips, piano roll, automation

Quick gestures

As in FL Studio, the arrangement opens things with no hunting through menus:

  • Double-click a track header (anywhere but its name): its instrument opens — the instrument's window (Unda, Pulsus II…) or a CLAP/VST3 plug-in's own window. Firmus and audio tracks, which have no window, show the inspector instead.
  • Click a track's name — on the letters themselves (the pointer becomes a text cursor) — to rename it in place: the name is selected, type the new one, Enter keeps it (one undo step), Esc puts back the old one. A click elsewhere on the header only selects the track, and typing with a track selected never renames it. (Mixer strips and rack pads rename on a double-click.)
  • Double-click a clip: the piano roll opens on it, the whole clip in view. A step pattern the rack shows with nothing lost — every note a short hit on a step, one pitch per channel (a drum kit may use all its pads; a melody on a synth opens the piano roll, as in FL) — opens in the channel rack instead, at the pattern's own grid and scrolled to its track. An automation clip opens in its large editor; an audio clip opens in the pitch editor (its gain, fades and file stay in the inspector).
  • Open editor — in the inspector, the mixer's menus or an instrument's frame — brings a window that is already open back from the tray (or the taskbar, for a plug-in instrument or effect) and to the front; while a plug-in's window is open its key reads Show. F8 and the channel rack's channel button do the same when the window is minimised, behind another tab or under another window, and close it only when it is already in front. (Cantus cannot bring forward a plug-in that floats a window of its own: its key reads Close.)

Clips in the arrangement

  • Create: double-click an empty place on a track (a 4-bar clip), or record.
  • Select: click; Ctrl+click adds; drag on empty space for a marquee; Esc clears.
  • Move: drag (also to another track). Alt+drag copies; Ctrl while dragging ignores the grid. Left/Right nudge by the grid, Shift by a bar.
  • Trim: drag a clip's edge — its content stays where it is. Slip: Shift+drag slides the content inside the clip.
  • Split at the playhead: Ctrl+E. Glue: Ctrl+J. Duplicate: Ctrl+D (press again to keep tiling). Mute: 0. Rename: F2. Colour: Ctrl+Shift+C.
  • Time selection: drag in the ruler's lower lane; the top strip is the loop. Shift+L loops the selection, Z zooms to it.
  • Audio clips have handles for fade in, fade out and gain (double-click one to reset it; right-click a fade for its curve) and a menu with Normalize, Reverse, Reset clip gain and Remove fades.
  • Crossfades: drag an audio clip so it starts over another and the overlap crossfades by itself, in the same undo step — the earlier clip fading out while the later one fades in, drawn as two curves across the overlap. Its square at the top of the overlap: drag it sideways to make the crossfade longer or shorter (both clips' edges move about its centre, as far as their audio goes); double-click it for the crossfade's editor (its curve drawn, No crossfade · Equal power · Linear · S-curve, Length in ms); right-click it for the same choices. Equal power (the default) keeps two different recordings level across the overlap; Linear and S-curve suit two takes of the same thing. An overlap with No crossfade plays both clips summed (dashed edges). Turn the automatic crossfades off (or choose their curve) in Settings ▸ Editing or the timeline's right-click menu (Automatic crossfades); the clip menu's Crossfade the overlap crosses (or uncrosses) the selection's overlaps by hand. A clip lying wholly inside another is layered over it, not crossed; clips that only meet are a butt join.
  • Clip gain envelopes: right-click an empty place in the arrangement ▸ Show clip gain envelopes (or the clip menu). Each audio clip shows a gain line — +12 dB at the top, 0 dB a quarter of the way down, −∞ at the bottom — and its waveform follows it. On a selected or hovered clip, press on the line to add a point and drag it (Shift: finely); double-click a point to delete it; drag the small circle half-way along a segment to bend it (double-click to straighten); right-click a point or the line for the segment's shape (the automation editor's six), Delete point or Reset gain envelope. The line belongs to the audio: slip the content and it moves with it, trim and it is hidden (not lost), split and both halves keep it, glue and the pieces' lines join, reverse and it plays mirrored with the audio. It works on top of the clip's gain and fades. MIDI clips have no gain line: their notes' velocity is their level.
  • Import audio: drag WAV, AIFF, FLAC or Ogg files onto the arrangement (they land at the pointer), or File ▸ Import audio…. A MIDI file adds its tracks.
  • Linked copies: a paste, Ctrl+D or Alt+drag makes the same clip again — edit one and every copy follows (notes, or an audio clip's gain, fades and reverse). A link mark and a ×N count show it. Make unique (Alt+U) takes the selection out of its group; Select linked copies (Shift+Alt+L); Link selection to this clip (Alt+L); Link to… (Alt+N) picks, by name or track, a linked group or a clip of its own to become. In the inspector, a linked clip's Link row shows how many copies it has and offers Link to…; renaming it in the Name row renames every copy. Right-click an empty place in the arrangement ▸ Copies are linked to make independent copies instead.
  • The clip browser (Alt+F5, or View ▸ Clip browser) shows every linked group and every clip of its own once, as tiles in the clip's colour with a miniature of what it holds; type to filter by name or track. Drag a tile onto the arrangement to place a linked copy there. Click a tile to choose it for the paint tool: with the arrangement focused, B (or 2) takes the tool out — a click places a linked copy, a drag paints copies end to end along the track, a right-drag (or Alt+drag) erases the copies of that clip only; each stroke is one undo step; Esc puts the tool away. Double-click a tile to select all its copies.

The grid (the arrangement's toolbar) starts at one bar; choose finer values for detail. Scroll with the wheel, Shift+wheel for time, Ctrl+wheel to zoom.

The tempo track and markers

A song can change tempo and metre as it goes, and carry named markers (round 29).

  • The tempo track is a lane under the ruler (View ▸ Tempo track, or the ruler's menu): each point is a tempo, held flat to the next point or ramping straight to it. Double-click the lane to add a point (on the grid, at the tempo where you clicked); drag a point to move it (Shift for fine tempo steps, Ctrl off the grid); Alt+click turns a point's ramp on or off; double-click a point to type its tempo; right-click for Ramp to the next point, Remove the point and Remove the tempo track. The header beside the lane shows the tempo at the playhead.
  • The top bar's tempo shows the tempo at the playhead (inside a ramp, its value there). Dragging it and the wheel (a notch, one BPM) move the point the playhead is under by that much — inside a ramp, the ramp's start; a double-click (120) or TAP set it — the song's tempo when there is no tempo track.
  • Metre changes are chips on the ruler before a bar's number ("7/8"). Right-click the marker lane or the ruler ▸ Add a metre change here (at bar 1 it opens the song's own metre); click a chip to edit its signature, drag it along the bar lines between its neighbours, right-click to remove it. The bars after a change keep their numbers; notes never move. The top bar's metre edits the metre in force at the playhead.
  • Markers appear in a lane under the loop lane. Alt+M adds one at the playhead (or double-click the lane, or right-click the ruler ▸ Add a marker here); click a marker to put the playhead there; double-click it to rename it; drag it along the grid; markers on one tick sit side by side; right-click for Rename…, Colour…, Loop to the next marker, Select to the next marker, Export to the next marker… and Remove the marker. Alt+, and Alt+. go to the previous and next marker; Alt+Shift+O loops between the markers around the playhead. The lane's menu lists every marker under Go to.
  • Export between markers: File ▸ Export audio… ▸ What ▸ Markers, then pick the two markers by name (two at different places: the dialog says so otherwise, and never exports the whole song instead).
  • Everything follows the tempo: notes, audio clips, automation, modulators, synced effects, the click (the first beat of every bar accented; a bar of 7/8 counted in eighths) and plug-ins made by others. An unwarped audio clip plays at its natural speed from the point of its file at its start: split it, trim it or comp it anywhere and every piece plays on where it was; move or copy clips elsewhere and each lands on its own bar, playing the same part of its file (the pieces of one take moved together still meet). A tempo change never changes what a take plays where it comes in, and the pieces of one take move together, so a split never jumps; a change inside a take lets the rest of it run on at its own speed — warp it to keep it on its bars. A reversed clip keeps the point of its file it lands on, at its end. While audio records the tempo stays as it is — undo and redo of a tempo change too; recording notes leaves it free.
  • MIDI files carry the whole tempo map out (a ramp as a tempo change every sixteenth note — a very long one, over 1 024 beats, in fewer, longer steps, each its stretch's average — and every marker, those at the end too) and in — importing with Set the project to … BPM in … keeps the file's tempo changes, metre changes and markers: at the song's start they are the song's; dropped at a later bar they start there (the bar line nearest the drop), the song keeping its own tempo and metre before it. DAWproject files carry the tempo track, the metre changes and the markers both ways. A file's tempo points, metre changes or markers past a million beats are left out, and the import says so.

The pitch editor

Double-click an audio clip — or its menu ▸ Edit pitch, or Edit pitch in the inspector — and its take opens in the piano roll's place, Melodyne's way: each note is a blob on the row it sounds, as thick as the voice is loud, with the pitch curve running through it (what was sung, dim, under what will sound). Sibilants and breaths are marked along the bottom. The take is analysed beside the window the first time (a second or so per minute of audio; a recorded take is analysed as soon as it lands) and the result is kept in Cantus's cache, so it opens at once next time. The file is never changed: the clip plays a render of your edits, made beside the window, and while a render is on its way you hear the take as it was (never silence). Export waits for every render.

  • Edit tool: click a note to select it (Shift adds; drag on empty ground for several). Drag it up or down to move its pitch — on the notes of the key (Snap: Scale), on semitones, or anywhere (Free; hold Alt for cents). Drag it sideways to move it in time, or drag one of its ends to stretch it: only the gaps beside it give way, its neighbours stay exactly as sung. Double-click a note to pull its centre onto its note (again to let it go). Up/Down move the selection a step (Shift: an octave, Alt: 10 cents), Ctrl+A selects every note, Ctrl+J joins, Q quantises, Esc clears.
  • Split tool: click inside a note to cut it in two; Join puts neighbours together.
  • The note (the panel below): what the selection sounds and was sung, and its centre (how far its centre is pulled onto its note, keeping its natural drift and vibrato), drift (the slow wander: 100 % as sung, 0 % flat), vibrato (0–200 %), formant (the voice's colour, up or down, the pitch kept) and gain.
  • The macros act on the selection, or on every note when none is selected: Correct pitch by an amount (each note that far toward its note — a note off the key toward its nearest note; 0 % leaves it alone), Correct drift by an amount, and Quantise each note's start to what Quantise to on the bar says: a grid, or another track's notes (each note onto the nearest one within an eighth note).
  • Key: found from the notes; change it on the bar (Tonus's scales). Original compares with the take as sung (A/B) — the inspector has the same switch. Reset undoes every edit on the selection. ▶ plays from the first selected note. Every edit is one undo step.
  • MIDI: drag it onto an instrument track in the arrangement and the melody lands there, at the voice's place; click it for a new track. Double: a second voice a third or a fifth above in the key, from the same take, on a track of its own. MIDI out and Double are also in the audio clip's right-click menu.
  • When the editor is narrow, what the bar has no room for is in its … menu, and the panel under the notes scrolls sideways.

The pitch lane: right-click an audio track's header ▸ Pitch lane shows its notes in a lane under it, in the arrangement — drag one up or down to move it, double-click it to pull it onto its note — for small fixes without opening the editor.

Formants are kept when a note moves (measured within 3 dB at ±3 semitones). It works on one voice or one instrument line at a time; chords are not split into notes.

Warping and stretching audio

Warping makes an audio clip play in the project's time: faster or slower than it was recorded, without changing its pitch, and following every tempo change of the tempo track. The file is never changed: the clip plays a render made beside the window.

  • Loops warp themselves. Import a loop (from File ▸ Import audio…, a drop from Windows or from the browser) and Cantus finds its tempo, its beats and its first downbeat; a file of a whole number of bars (or one whose WAV carries its tempo) is warped at once to the project's tempo, in the same undo step as the import (one step for every file of a drop). Longer files, every take you record and every take brought back after a crash play at their natural speed until you warp them: a recording plays as it was played.
  • Warp a clip yourself: right-click it ▸ Warp ▸ Follow the project tempo (or Follow in the inspector or the editor) — nothing moves until you move a marker, even under a tempo ramp — Detect tempo (markers on every bar it found), or Warp from here (its tempo counted from the beat where you right-clicked; in the inspector, from the playhead). Turning Follow off plays the clip at its natural speed and keeps its markers for when you turn it on again; turning it off, resetting the warp or laying it again never moves what the clip plays at its start (nor on its linked copies). A linked copy under another tempo than its copies' is made unique when you first warp it, so they keep playing as they did. Set the project tempo from this clip goes the other way: the clip's tempo (what the analysis found, while it does not follow) becomes the tempo where it starts.
  • Warp markers pin a moment of the audio to a beat. Any audio clip under the pointer or selected shows its transients (the hits found in it) as ticks along a band at its top; a clip that follows the tempo also shows its markers as gold flags — the ones on its two ends too — and takes clicks on the band. Double-click the band to add a marker (on a transient, it lands on it); drag in the band to add one and move it at once. Drag a marker to move that moment of the audio to another beat — it snaps to the grid (hold Ctrl for free) and the audio between its neighbours stretches to fit. Hold Alt while dragging to slide the audio under the marker instead. Double-click a marker, or right-click it ▸ Remove warp marker, or click it and press Del, to remove it. On a clip that does not follow the tempo the band takes no click: dragging near its top moves the clip, as anywhere else on it.
  • Stretch a whole clip: hold Alt and drag its right edge (Shift still slips). A linked copy stretches on its own (it leaves its group).
  • The clip editor's Warp tab: double-click the clip (or Edit warp) — the editor has two tabs, Pitch and Warp, and opens in the one you used last. The Warp tab shows the clip large on a bar ruler, with the same gestures on any audio clip, and a bar with the mode, its own snap grid (1/4 to 1/32, or free), the transposition, Formants, Follow, the clip's tempo (½, ×2 when it was counted at half or double), Detect tempo, Quantise… and Reset warp — what does nothing in the clip's mode is shown greyed; in a narrow window the bar takes a second row. Ctrl+wheel (or a pinch) zooms in on the clip about the pointer, the wheel (or the bar under it) scrolls, and Q opens Quantise audio.
  • Modes (the inspector's Warp section, or the bar) — choosing one does not warp the clip:
  • Complex — anything: full mixes, pads, chords. Keeps the attacks sharp, a chord's voices steady and the stereo image in place.
  • Rhythm — drums and percussive loops: each hit is played exactly as recorded, only moved; Slice ends chooses what fills the gap after a hit when it is slowed down (Fade, Loop, Mirror: back and forth).
  • Melodic — one voice or one instrument line: the cleanest for a solo, with the voice's colour kept when transposed.
  • Re-pitch — like a turntable: the speed and the pitch change together (no processing artefacts; Transpose is then off).
  • Transpose shifts the clip by semitones and cents without changing its time (a clip that does not follow the tempo keeps its natural speed); Formants ▸ Keep keeps a voice's colour when it is transposed (no "chipmunk"). The level stays as it was.
  • Detected and Confidence in the inspector: the tempo and bars the analysis found, and how sure it is. A marker's hint gives the speed the audio plays at after it, against the tempo in force there.
  • Quantise audio… (the clip menu or the inspector, on every audio clip selected with the one you chose): pulls the transients toward a grid with a Strength, only the stronger ones if you choose (Transients over) — one undo step for every clip; only the hits you hear move (a loop's own period; a reversed clip's on the lines where they play), the rest keeps its speed.
  • Bounce warped audio writes the clip's window as it sounds now to a new file beside the project's audio, and the clip then plays that file, unwarped (one undo step). Reset warp puts the clip back to its natural speed.

While a render is on its way (a fraction of a second for a loop, a few seconds for a long file) you hear a quick preview at the right timing and pitch — a clip transposed for the first time plays its own audio meanwhile; one whose markers move its audio waits a moment for its preview, never playing at the wrong bar — and the change from one to the next is crossfaded once. Export, stems, freezing, bouncing and a modulator's bake wait for the full render; if one ever takes too long, the status line says so when the job is done. Warped clips travel in DAWproject files with their markers and mode (a file Cantus wrote comes back with its warp exactly as it was); a clip at its natural speed travels as a file placed in seconds, so it keeps its speed in any application. Opening or dropping a .dawproject reads it in the background (its audio copied to disk as it goes, a file referenced beside it found there); it opens once nothing is being recorded or dragged, and never over another project opened meanwhile.

Automation

Right-click any knob, fader or effect control ▸ Create automation clip, or move a control and press Alt+A (automate the last thing you touched). The clip covers the time selection (else the loop, else four bars) on a lane under its track. On the lane: drag points, drag the ring between two points to bend the curve, click the line to add a point, double-click a point to delete it, right-click it for its shape (single or double curve, hold, stairs, pulse, wave). Double-click the clip for the large editor, with a drawing tool and articulators (flip, smooth, random, scale, LFO). An automated control shows a dot.

Hosted plug-ins are automated like Cantus's own: right-click a parameter in a plug-in's generic editor (or in a hosted instrument's Parameters list in the inspector), or move it in the plug-in's own window and press Alt+A. Their changes reach the plug-in at their exact sample inside the audio block.

Recording automation. While you record, every control you move — in Cantus or in a plug-in's own window — is written into an automation clip over the stretch you moved it in (one undo step when the pass ends). Switch it off in the record options (Automation). (This is the Latch mode below, for every track in Read.)

Writing automation while the song plays: Read, Write, Touch, Latch. Each track has an automation mode — the badge at the right of its header (R, W, T, L; click it, or Alt+Shift+W for the selected track), the inspector's Automation row; the master's is in its menu on the mixer. The automation arm in the top bar (the envelope key after loop) makes them write; its ▾ sets every track at once and the Touch glide.

ModeWhile the song plays, armed
Read (R)Plays the automation. Moving a control writes nothing
Write (W)Overwrites everything the track automates, from play to stop, each held where it was until you move it. After the pass the track switches to Touch

What you write becomes ordinary automation clips over exactly the stretch you wrote; the control goes back to where it was before the first clip, and the whole pass — with the moves of your hand — is one undo step. It works for every automatable control: faders, pans, sends, effects' and instruments' parameters, plug-ins' parameters moved in their own windows, modulator depths, and controllers mapped to any of them. Round a loop, each pass writes over the last where it touches; with punch on, only the punch range is written.

Modulators

A modulator moves parameters for you: an LFO (five shapes, synced to the song or free in Hz, restarted by notes if you like), an envelope (attack, decay, sustain, release — opened by the track's notes or by an audio level), a follower (the level of any strip: duck a pad under a kick), steps (up to 32, synced, with glide) and a random source (held or smooth, repeatable from its seed). Each modulator drives any number of parameters, each with its own depth, plus or minus; a parameter may take several, and they add up on top of its automation.

  • Where they live: the inspector's Modulators section for the selected track, its channel, or the master (click the band's +). Each modulator shows its movement as a picture, its settings, its amount, and one row per parameter it drives: drag the depth, bake it, or disconnect it.
  • Connect: right-click any knob, fader or plug-in parameter ▸ Modulate… and pick a modulator or make a new one already connected; or drag a modulator's handle (the grip at the left of its title) onto any knob; or press its map key and drag any knob — the drag sets that modulator's depth there.
  • Depth on the knob: hold Alt and drag a modulated knob to change the depth of its modulation (only the drag: a double-click still resets the value). Modulated knobs wear coloured rings (the depth, in the modulator's colour), a moving dot where the parameter is now, and a violet mark.
  • Everywhere: every knob of Cantus's instruments and effects, the mixer's faders, pans and widths (the master's too), every send's level (its knob and row on the desk, its cable's level, the inspector's send and receive rows; a send's right-click menu has Automate and Modulate… for it), the inspector's pinned strip and a hosted plug-in's parameters take a modulator the same way. So does a modulator's own amount: let a random source move how deep an LFO goes (one level of nesting). Chorda's tuning (A4, stretch, stiffness) is set, never automated or modulated.
  • Bake to automation: a route's bake key (or Modulate… ▸ Bake to automation) turns that modulator's effect into an automation clip over the time selection (else the loop, else the song) and removes the route, in one undo step.

Synced modulators follow the song position, so playing from bar 5 hears what a full play hears there; free ones start with playback. Exports sound exactly as playback.

Note effects: arpeggiator, chords, scale

A track can change the notes it is given before its instrument plays them: an arpeggiator, chords and a scale, in a chain, in the order you set (FL's channel Arp and Chord, Ableton's MIDI effects). They act on the clips' notes and on what you play live, alike — while the song plays and while it is stopped.

  • Add one: select the track; in the inspector's Note effects section click the band's + (or the empty row) and pick Arpeggiator, Chords or Scale. A track holds up to eight. Each one's title row has earlier / later (its place in the chain), its on/off light and remove; click its name to fold it.
  • Arpeggiator: Order (up, down, up-down, down-up, as played, chord, random), Octaves (1–4), Rate (1/1 to 1/64, dotted . and triplet T, synced to the tempo), Gate (how long each note sounds, 5–200 % of a step), Swing (MPC-style, 50–75 %), Hold (the notes keep playing once your keys are up, until you play a new chord), Fixed vel. with its Velocity (or each note's own), and Seed (what Random draws from: the same pattern every time you play). The first step plays where the chord starts; a chord on the grid arpeggiates on the grid.
  • Chords: Chord (major, minor, dim, aug, sus2, sus4, 7, maj7, m7, m(maj7), m7b5, dim7, 6, m6, add9, m(add9), 9, maj9, m9, 5, 5+8, 8va — or Triad of the key and Seventh of the key, the key's own chord on each degree: I, ii, iii … in a major key), Inversion (0–3) and Voicing (close, open — the second voice up an octave — or wide — open, the root an octave down).
  • Scale: notes Outside the key are moved to its nearest note (Snap to nearest; ties go down) or not played (Drop).
  • Which key: chords from the key and the scale read the song's key (below), unless you switch on their Own key and choose its Root and Scale.
  • Every parameter is automatable and modulatable: right-click its row ▸ Create automation clip or Modulate…, or drop a modulator's handle on it, as on any knob.
  • What hears them: playback, export, stems, freeze and bounce all play the notes the chain makes — sample for sample the same. An insert that listens to the track's notes (Porta's MIDI trigger) hears them too; a modulator opened or restarted by the track's notes (an envelope, a retriggered LFO) follows the notes as written, before the chain — one opening per chord, not per arpeggio step. Recording keeps what you played (the keys), not the arpeggio.
  • Bake to notes: on a MIDI clip's inspector, Bake to notes replaces the clip with one holding the notes the chain plays from it (its groove included) — one undo step. The new clip plays past the note effects (its Note effects ▸ Bypass light is on), so they do not act twice; switch it off to send a clip through them again.

The song's key, and the piano roll's scale

Set the song's key on the piano roll's toolbar: click Key and pick a root and a scale (major, minor, harmonic and melodic minor, the modes, the pentatonics, blues, whole tone, the diminished scales, Phrygian dominant, Hungarian minor, Japanese, chromatic) — or No key. It is saved with the song.

  • Scale: the key's rows are lit — the rows outside it a shade darker, the root's rows tinted — and the keyboard marks the key's notes (the root wider).
  • Fold: the piano roll shows only the key's rows (and any row a note of the clip is on, so nothing hides). Moving a note one row moves it one note of the key.
  • Snap: drawing, painting, dragging and transposing (Up/Down) keep notes in the key: a drawn note lands on the key's nearest note, a dragged one on the next note of the key the way it moved, Up/Down move one degree of the key (Shift: an octave).

Grooves

A groove gives a clip a feel — notes late or early, accented, longer or shorter — without touching its notes. In a MIDI clip's inspector, Groove opens the list: the built-in swings (8ths and 16ths, MPC-style, 50–75 %) and the project's own grooves.

  • Amounts: Timing (0–150 %: past 100 % it exaggerates), Accents (how much of the groove's velocities) and Lengths. The notes stay as you wrote them; playback, export and everything else hear them grooved.
  • Commit writes the groove into the notes and takes it off the clip (one undo step).
  • Extract groove puts a clip's feel into the project's grooves — a bar, on a 16th grid: each step's average lateness, accent and length. On an audio clip it reads the clip's transients (the ones Warp finds). The new groove then appears in every clip's list.
  • Patterns: the channel rack's toolbar has a Groove field: the choice goes to every clip of the current pattern at once.
  • Linked clips share their groove, as they share their notes.

A MIDI file export asks which notes to write: As played (each clip's groove applied and each track's note effects written out note by note — the file plays what you hear), or As written (the clips' notes, no groove, no note effects).

The score: notation, MusicXML, PDF and printing

Every note clip and every MIDI track can be read as an engraved score, made as Dorico, MuseScore and Sibelius make one — the notes read onto values the metre shows, tied where a beat or the half bar must be seen, beamed by the beat, spelled in the song's key with accidentals per bar, in the SMuFL font Bravura.

  • The staff under the piano roll: the piano roll's toolbar has a Score switch (Alt+S). The clip's part appears as one long staff under the roll; drag its top edge to make it taller. It follows the playhead while playing (Follow), shows the piano roll's selection in the selection colour, and scrolls with the mouse wheel; Ctrl + wheel or − / + make the staff smaller or larger.
  • The Score pane (View ▸ Score, Alt+Shift+S): the selected track's whole part, every clip of its playing lane, in systems that wrap to the pane's width and scroll down.
  • Reading, not moving: the score rounds the notes for reading only — the notes you hear never move. Each beat chooses straight values or triplets (sextuplets and quintuplets too) by what the notes fit; Read as forces a grid (eighths, sixteenths, 32nds, triplets, sextuplets) when a passage reads better one way.
  • The part's settings (in the score's toolbar; each is one step of the undo history): Clef (Auto picks the clef with the fewest ledger lines; octave clefs for parts that live very high or low; very high or low passages get an 8va / 8vb line), Staves (Auto gives a part that spans both hands a grand staff), Split (a grand staff follows the hands — a melody that steps below middle C stays in the right hand — or splits at a fixed note), Pitch (a transposing instrument: a B♭ clarinet or trumpet is written a tone up, an E♭ alto saxophone a sixth up, a horn in F a fifth up; the key signature moves with it), Dynamics (pp … ff written from the notes' velocities), and the part's name (empty is the track's name).
  • Tempo and metre come from the tempo map: a ♩ = 120 mark at the start and at every tempo change (a dotted quarter in 6/8 and 12/8; accel. or rit. where a ramp starts), and a time signature at every metre change.
  • Editing on the staff: click a note to select it (Shift+click adds). With the score clicked last, Up / Down move the selection a step of the song's key (a semitone without a key), Ctrl+Up / Ctrl+Down an octave, Delete removes it. Input (or N) enters notes MuseScore's way: 3–7 choose the value (a sixteenth to a whole note), . a dot, the letters A–G put a note at the cursor — in the key, in the octave nearest the last note — and move the cursor on; a click on the staff moves the cursor; Esc stops (note input also stops at the clip's end). In a transposed part the letters are the written notes. Outside note input, Shift+Up / Shift+Down and Esc keep their usual meaning (transpose an octave, clear the selection). Every edit is a normal command: Ctrl+Z undoes it.
  • Files (the score's Files menu): Export MusicXML… writes the clip's or the track's part, Export the song as MusicXML… every MIDI track as a part (MusicXML 4.0, which MuseScore, Dorico, Sibelius and Finale open; each note's velocity goes with it). Export PDF (A4 / Letter)… writes vector pages with the fonts embedded, the project's name as the title and the export's Artist tag as the composer. Import MusicXML… puts each part of a .musicxml file on a new track as one clip (a compressed .mxl must be unpacked first); into a song with no notes yet, the file's tempo, metres and key come with it, while a song with notes keeps its own.
  • Printing: Print… makes the PDF and hands it to your system's printing: on Windows the default PDF application prints it (Edge, Acrobat, SumatraPDF…; with none set up to print, the PDF opens so you can print it there); on Linux and macOS it is sent to the default printer with lp (CUPS), or opens in the PDF viewer when CUPS is not there.

The session view: launching clips and scenes

The Session pane is a clip launcher, as in Ableton Live and Bitwig: the same tracks as the arrangement, side by side as columns, and scenes as rows. Each cell (a slot) holds a clip you can start and stop while the song plays, in time; a scene starts a whole row at once. The tracks, the mixer and the instruments are the arrangement's — the session only decides which clip each track plays.

  • Switching: Tab brings the Session pane forward, or the arrangement back (Live's key); the top bar's window keys have a Session key, and View ▸ Session does the same. What you chose stays chosen: a session clip you click is the piano roll's clip.
  • What you see: each column's head shows the track's name and colour, its arm (●), mute (M), solo (S) and back to arrangement keys. A slot with a clip shows its name, a small view of its notes (or its waveform) and, while it plays, a bar along its foot. Its ▶ is the launcher: green while the clip plays, blinking while it waits for the bar (or the grid you chose), red while it records. An empty slot shows a small square — it stops its track — or, when the track is armed, a red dot that records into it. The scenes are at the right, with their names and, when a scene sets them, its tempo and metre ("128 · 4/4"); under them Stop all, and under each column its stop button.
  • Launching: click a clip's ▶ (or press Enter on the slot the cursor is on) — it starts on the next bar, or on the Launch quantisation at the top of the pane (none, 8 bars … 1/32, triplets). Click a scene's ▶ (Shift+Enter) to start its row; a scene that sets a tempo or a metre changes the song's when it starts. Ctrl+Enter stops the cursor's track, Ctrl+Shift+Enter stops everything. A track playing a session clip leaves its arrangement silent until you press its back to arrangement key (or Back to arr. at the top, Alt+B, for every track).
  • Editing in the grid (each edit is one undo step): double-click an empty slot to make a one-bar MIDI clip, a clip to open it in the piano roll; drag a clip to another slot to move it (Alt or Ctrl: a copy), or onto the arrangement to put it there; drag clips from the arrangement onto a slot to bring them into the session (each track's into its own column); drop a sample from the browser on an empty slot. With the pane clicked, the arrows move the slot cursor (Shift: eight at a time), F2 renames, Ctrl+Shift+C changes the colour, Ctrl+D duplicates (a clip into the next free slot below; a scene under itself), Ctrl+C / Ctrl+X / Ctrl+V copy, cut and paste clips, Delete deletes, Ctrl+Shift+L turns the clip's loop on or off. Ctrl+I inserts a scene, Ctrl+Alt+I captures one — a new scene holding a copy of whatever plays now. Right-click a clip, a slot or a scene for the rest: put a clip in the arrangement at the playhead, consolidate a track's clips into one, remove an empty slot's stop button, make a scene set the song's tempo or metre, move a scene up or down. Double-click a scene's name to rename it.
  • The clip panel (at the foot of the pane; the key at the top right shows or hides it; drag its top edge to make it taller): Launch — the mode (Trigger starts the clip, Gate plays it while you hold the button, Toggle starts and stops it, Repeat retriggers it while held), its own quantisation (Global follows the pane's), Legato (it takes over the position of the clip it replaces), and how much a pad's velocity sets its level. Loop — the loop on or off, the start marker, where the loop starts and how long it is (as Live writes them: bar.beat.sixteenth; drag a value, Shift for fine steps, double-click to reset). Follow actions — what happens after the clip has played: action A or B (stop, play again, previous, next, first, last, any, other, or jump to a scene), the chance of A against B, after the clip's own length (Linked) or a time, and how many times. Follow actions draw their chances from the session's seed, so a performance plays the same way again. Envelopes — choose a parameter of the clip's track (its volume, pan, its strip, its effects, its instrument, or the last one you moved) and draw its curve over the clip's loop: a click adds a point, a drag moves it, a double-click or a right-click deletes it. The clip moves that parameter while it plays and hands it back to the track's automation when it stops. A short pane hides the panel so the grid keeps its rows: make the pane taller.
  • Recording into a slot: arm the track (●), then click the red dot of an empty slot (or press Alt+Enter): it records from the next bar (after the count-in when the transport is stopped); click it again, or launch the slot, to stop — the new clip plays on.
  • Recording the session into the arrangement: switch Arr. record on (Ctrl+Alt+R) and play: everything you launch and stop, and every follow action, is written into the arrangement exactly as you heard it, with the clips' envelopes as automation and the scenes' tempos in the tempo track. When you switch it off, the whole take is one undo step.
  • MIDI controllers: in the MIDI learn mode (Ctrl+Shift+M) every slot, scene launcher, stop button, Stop all, Arr. record, Back to arr. and the Launch quantisation is outlined: click one, then press a pad or turn a knob. A pad launches its slot and, let go, releases it (a Gate clip stops); its light shows the slot's state. The scene cursor (Previous scene, Next scene) and every session action can be mapped too.
  • Launchpad and APC grids: plug in a Launchpad Mini MK3, Launchpad X, Launchpad Pro MK3, APC mini mk2 or APC40 mkII and it becomes a grid of the session — found by its port's name (or chosen under Settings ▸ MIDI or at the foot of the controller mappings pane, where you can also turn it off). Its pads launch the slots of the columns and rows it shows (the session ring, drawn as an orange box in the pane), lit in the clips' colours — green and pulsing while playing, flashing while waiting, red while recording; the scene buttons launch rows; the arrows move the ring (Shift: a page); the stop buttons stop tracks (on a Mini or an X, the lowest scene button turns the bottom row of pads into stop buttons, and the Session button stops everything; on an APC mini, Shift with the first four track buttons moves the ring and Shift with the last scene button stops everything). A grid's pads never play notes on the selected track.

The session: how launching, follow actions and recording work

The session view's own page tells you where everything is; this one tells you what happens when you press it.

Same tracks, same mixer. A column of the session is a track of the song: the same instrument, the same mixer strip, the same automation. A clip launched on a track takes over that track's arrangement until you press Back to Arrangement (on the track, or for every track) — even after the clip stops, the track stays with the session, as in Live.

When a launch happens. A clip waits for its launch quantisation — its own, or the session's global one (a bar by default) — and starts exactly on that boundary, to the sample. A launch pressed just before a boundary, too late for the engine to have it ready there (a few milliseconds to tens of them, by your audio buffer), still plays on it — a moment late, caught up to where it would be, never a whole bar later; and so does one pressed a 32nd note or less after a boundary (on a launch grid of a 32nd or finer, a quarter of a grid step). With the transport stopped, a launch starts it at once, from the playhead. A scene launches its whole row on the global quantisation, and sets its tempo and metre there (the metre on the next bar line).

Following Link, MIDI clock or time code. A launch from stop waits for the source to start: Link's next quantum, or the master's Start — however long that is; the clip shows as queued meanwhile, and the stop button calls it off. If the master starts from another place (MIDI clock's Start is from the top), the clip plays from there. Following Link, a scene with a tempo sets the tempo of the whole Link session when it takes effect, as in Live. A relock of Link, or the master moving the playhead, does not end what the session plays.

Launch modes. Trigger starts the clip (again) on each press. Gate plays it while held and stops it at the boundary after you let go. Toggle starts it, and stops it when pressed again. Repeat restarts it on every boundary while held. Legato makes a launched clip take over where the clip it replaces had got to, instead of starting from its start marker.

Follow actions. After its follow time (a time, or linked to its own length, times its count) a clip does action A or B — stop, play again, the previous, next, first, last, any or another clip of its column, or jump to a row — with the chance you set for A. The chances are drawn from the session's seed: the same performance plays the same way every time, and an export renders exactly what you heard. Change the seed for another performance.

Recording into a slot. On an armed track, the record button on an empty slot records from the next boundary (after the count-in, from stop) to the bar line after you stop it — or for the session's fixed length — and the new clip plays on at once, looping. MIDI is quantised by the session's record quantisation. Recorded over a looping MIDI clip that plays, the notes go into that clip (overdub). The take is kept like any recording: a crash loses nothing.

Recording the session into the arrangement. With arrangement record on, everything you launch, stop and every follow action is written into the arrangement exactly where you heard it — the clips, their envelopes as automation, the scenes' tempos — as one undo step, when you switch it off or stop. The arrangement then plays exactly what you heard, to the bit.

Good to know. A frozen track's clips do not launch (unfreeze it first). Freeze, bounce and export render the arrangement: record a performance into it first to keep it. Stopping the transport, or jumping the playhead, ends the performance. A tempo change keeps an unwarped audio clip playing the same stretch of its file; a warped one follows the tempo.

Audio to MIDI. Right-click an audio clip ▸ Convert to MIDI… turns a melody, a chord part or a drum loop into notes on a new track: see Convert audio to MIDI.

6. Mix: the mixer, inserts and sends

Each track feeds a strip of the mixer (F9); strips feed the master at the left, or another strip (a bus). A strip has its level meter, mute and solo, pan, fader, phase and swap, width, a three-band EQ, its inserts and its sends. Click to select, Ctrl+click to add, Shift+click for a run; dragging one fader of a selection moves them all. With the mixer focused, M mutes, S solos, Alt+Left/Alt+Right move the strip. Ctrl+wheel changes the strips' width; at Extra large each strip shows a one-line summary of its chain (click it to open the inspector).

  • Inserts (up to ten per strip): click an empty slot to choose an effect in the device picker, or right-click it for the quick list. On a filled slot, click the lamp to bypass it and the name to open its window; right-click to swap, move or remove it. In the inspector, each insert has a Mix ring (dry/wet).
  • Sends: select a strip, then look at the Sends row of the others — click the ▲ of the strip you want to send to (a reverb return, a parallel compressor); a knob then sets the send's level. Each send taps after the fader (POST, the default) or before it (PRE) — click its PRE/POST chip; a PRE send keeps sounding when you pull the fader down.
  • Cables: each strip has a port. Drag from it to another strip, or click it and then the destination (Esc cancels): plain makes a send at 0 dB, Ctrl makes it the strip's only output, Alt makes it a sidechain key. The selected strip's cables (or all, or none — the switch above the desk, with a filter for which kinds) are drawn in a band under the desk; click a cable to select it, drag its end to move it, Delete removes it. A doubled path is allowed and marked. Every change is one undo step.
  • Routing: right-click a strip ▸ Routing ▸ Output or Send to, Shift+F10 for the selected strip's route menu, or select several strips and Create submix from selected. In a cable's or a send's route menu, Level ▸ Type a level… takes a level in dB (−∞ to 0; Enter or a click elsewhere sets it, in one undo step; Esc cancels). The routing matrix (Alt+F9) shows every source against every destination: click a cell to route, drag for the level (Shift for fine), Ctrl+drag copies down a column. Strips and groups move by dragging or with Alt+Left/Alt+Right; a track header dropped on a strip plays into it.
  • Device windows have Swap, minimise (to the tray in the status bar), maximise and close. F8 opens the selected track's instrument (or brings it to the front, or closes it when it is already in front), Esc closes the device you used last, F12 closes every floating one.

Solo follows the routing: a soloed strip is heard with what feeds it and what it feeds.

Freeze and bounce in place

Freeze turns a heavy track into a recording of itself, so its instrument and effects stop using the processor — and gives it back, exactly, when you unfreeze it.

  • Freeze a track: right-click its header ▸ Freeze track, Alt+Shift+F, or the inspector's Freeze row. A track on a strip of its own freezes with the strip: its instrument, clips and the strip's inserts and EQ. A track that shares its strip freezes its own instrument and clips. Freeze a strip (a bus, a return) from the strip's menu: it freezes with everything it gathers.
  • It renders first (a card at the corner shows how far, with Stop); the song keeps playing and nothing changes until it is done. Then the track is frozen in one undo step: a snowflake on its header and its strip, its clips frosted.
  • A frozen track or strip is still mixed: its fader, pan, width, mute, solo and send levels — and their automation — stay live, and so do the mute and solo on the header of a track frozen with its own strip (its level and balance are inside the render: mix it with the strip's fader). Unless a modulator inside what it freezes follows it (or the bus it plays into): then its mix is part of its sound and is locked too — the card says so. Anything that would change its sound (its notes, its instrument, its inserts, the tempo, a strip moved among the ones it gathers) is refused, and the card says how to give it back: Unfreeze (the same menu entries, the same key). A knob turned in a frozen plug-in's own window goes back where it was, with the same card — so does a preset or a sample loaded from its own browser. Unfrozen, it plays live again, to the bit.
  • What also plays somewhere else keeps playing: freezing a bus does not silence a strip that keys its compressor and plays to the master, nor a return the strip that sends to it. Solo stays a listening aid — a freeze is made as if nothing were soloed; with something soloed inside what you freeze, unsolo it first. A solo that silences part of what a frozen strip sounds with (one of the tracks or strips heard through it) plays that strip live while it lasts; a solo that silences all of it — a solo elsewhere — only mutes it, without waking its plug-ins. Saving a project moves a frozen take into its Audio folder and keeps it frozen.
  • Its plug-ins are unloaded while it is frozen and come back with their settings. A freeze needs the plug-ins it renders (a bypassed one is not needed): one that is not loaded is named, and nothing is frozen. So does a file it plays that is not there yet (a sound pack downloading, a drive unplugged; a take on a comp lane below, or a muted clip, does not count): the freeze names it, and a frozen track waits for its files before its render is made again. A track whose plug-ins change their latency during the song (an automated look-ahead or oversampling) cannot be frozen nor bounced in place: the status line says so.
  • Renders are kept in the project's own folder inside a Freeze folder beside it (Freeze/Song for Song.cantus; in Cantus's own cache for a project not saved yet), named by what they hold: freezing again what did not change is instant, and a project opened without them (on another computer, at another sample rate) plays live while they are made again in the background. Save as takes them along — copied in the background; closing Cantus while it copies keeps the window open, with a card, until the copy is done (Close now skips it; what was not copied is found where it was next time). A project renamed or copied beside the original finds its renders in the original's folder, and its next save brings them into its own. Renders no undo can bring back are removed from the project's folder when you save; other projects' renders beside it are never touched.
  • With the loop on, a frozen track plays its recording straight through: at the jump back to the loop's start you hear what led into that bar, not a reverb's tail from the loop's end (as in other DAWs; unfreeze it to hear the jump exactly).

Bounce in place renders to an audio clip, on a new track right under the original, at the same place:

  • Bounce in place (track menu, Alt+Shift+B): this track through its strip's inserts; the new track gets a strip of its own with the old one's fader, pan, width and sends.
  • Bounce in place (source only): the instrument and clips before the track's level; the new track plays into the same strip, through the same inserts.
  • Bounce clips in place (the clip menu): only the selected clips (on several tracks, one bounce each); with a time selection — chosen after the clips, or with none selected — the keys bounce what plays inside it, its edges moved out to the notes held across them. A track header chosen last makes the keys bounce the whole track, a strip chosen in the mixer the whole strip; the master is never frozen nor bounced. Clips or a time range cannot be bounced while something hears the track's notes (bounce the whole track), and comp windows joined by a crossfade are bounced together or not at all. The clip menu's entries act on clips, never on a strip or the master chosen before: the clip you right-clicked, with the clips selected with it.
  • Bounce strip in place (the strip menu): everything the strip gathers; what was keyed from it, or followed it, follows the new strip. Unsolo what is soloed inside it first.
  • The original is muted — or removed, if you tick Remove the original after a bounce in the track menu (Cantus remembers your choice; a track whose notes something still hears is muted instead, and the result says so). Its level, fader and send rides go with the bounce, and so does its solo. Through a plug-in with latency, the new clip's audio sits on the grid and the new track keeps the delay (the inspector's Delay row; the key at its right takes it away), so those rides and anything keyed from it stay in time — and the clip plays in time wherever you move, copy or export it. The tail (a reverb's) is kept until it rings out. One undo step. If you edit what it renders while it renders, it is bounced again from your edit (an edit elsewhere does not restart it); Stop while its file is written leaves nothing. A track that monitors a live input — or one that keys it or plays notes into it — is not bounced (switch its monitoring off first).

7. Master: the master chain, Fervor, Latitudo, Culmen

The master has its own chain of up to ten inserts, run on the whole mix before the master fader. Click the master's head to see its chain in the inspector (The whole mix → the inserts → Audio output), or right-click its name ▸ Effects ▸ + Add effect.

The demos master in this order, and it is a good start:

  1. Aequalis (EQ): a high-pass under the sub, a little air.
  2. Multiplex or Comprimo: gentle glue.
  3. Latitudo (stereo): the preset Master: tighten the low end or Master: wider top, mono bass keeps the bass in mono and the top wide. Watch its correlation meter: it should stay above zero.
  4. Culmen (maximizer): choose a target — Streaming −14 (−14 LUFS, −1 dBTP), Apple −16, CD −9, Club −7, Broadcast −23 — then play the loudest part of the song and click Learn drive: Culmen listens, sets the drive, listens again and stops on the target, in one undo step. The Loudness card shows momentary, short-term and integrated loudness and the true peak; the verdict says On target or how many LU you are over or under. Culmen works in three bands plus the full band, into a true-peak brickwall at the ceiling; its Character (Clean, Punch, Loud, Bus, Safe) and its ten presets cover most masters.

Fervor (saturation) adds colour: Tape, Tube, Overdrive, Fuzz, Clip, Fold or a drawn Curve, with oversampling, tone, mix and Level match, so that louder never fools you. On the master, the presets Master clipper and Tube glue on the mid are the gentle ones.

The mastering assistant

Right-click the master's name ▸ Mastering assistant…, or open the master chain's + Add effect list: the first row is the assistant. It listens to your mix as it reaches the master and proposes a chain of Cantus's own effects to master it — nothing it does is hidden: what it proposes are ordinary inserts you can open and change.

  1. Choose where the song is going: Platform (‹ › steps through Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube, TIDAL, Amazon Music, SoundCloud, CD, Club and EBU R 128). The line under it says what the platform aims at — Spotify plays every song at −14 LUFS and wants the true peak at or under −1 dBTP — and the bottom of the panel says where that number comes from and when it was checked.
  2. Choose the Balance: a genre's curve (Pop, Rock, Electronic, Hip-hop, Acoustic, Classical), or Load reference… — a finished track you like the sound of (WAV, FLAC, MP3, OGG, AIFF). With a reference the assistant matches its tone and width; the platform still sets the loudness.
  3. Listen to the whole song or only the loop. If the master already has effects, choose whether the proposal replaces them or is added after them.
  4. Click Listen and propose. Cantus renders the mix (a song of three minutes takes as long as an export), measures it and tries the chain on it until the loudness lands on the target. Stop stops it.

The table shows, for the mix, the proposed master and the target: integrated loudness, true peak, loudness range, the loudest three seconds, width (how much side there is against the middle), what folding to mono loses, and the lowest correlation. The chart draws the balance per octave — bars for the mix, a line for the proposal, rings for the target. Under it, each unit of the chain and why it is there: an EQ toward the curve (at most ±3 dB a band; ±6 with a reference), gentle glue when the song's dynamics are wider than the genre keeps, stereo width or a mono bass when the mix folds badly to mono, and a true-peak limiter whose gain lands the platform's loudness under its ceiling.

A/B plays the mix (A) or the proposed master (B) from the same place, the louder one turned down to the other's loudness: you compare tone and dynamics, not level — louder always sounds better, and the A/B does not let it fool you. Nothing in the project changes while you compare.

Apply puts the chain on the master in one undo step (Edit ▸ Undo takes it all back). Then play the song and adjust anything by ear — every knob is yours.

Listening for AI agents

When you allow an assistant such as Claude to work in Cantus (Settings ▸ AI), it can listen too: audition renders a range — or a soloed part, or the mix without the master chain — and answers with the numbers above for the range and each section, the spectrum against a genre's curve, clicks at section changes, and a WAV of what it heard (in Cantus's own cache); analyse_tracks measures every track in the mix; masking_matrix says which tracks fight for the same frequencies, and where; set_mix_targets sets tracks to the loudness the agent asks for, in one undo step; master_assist, apply_master_chain and replace_master_chain (which asks you first: it takes the master's own effects out) are the mastering assistant above. Cantus keeps a copy of the song's state at each marker (or every eight bars) while it renders, so after the agent changes the last chorus it hears only the last chorus again — the same sound, to the sample, as playing the whole song. With a hosted plug-in in the project that copy cannot be made; the range then starts eight seconds early, and the answer says so. The prompt Master for streaming asks an agent to do all of it for a platform you name.

File ▸ Export audio… (Ctrl+Shift+E in the default keys) opens the export window: presets on the left, the choices on the right, and a line that says exactly what will be written.

Chorda: the modelled grand piano

Chorda is a grand piano computed as you play — no samples. Every key has its own one, two or three strings, struck by a felt hammer and ringing into a soundboard; the strings of the other keys ring along when the dampers let them. So it answers the way a piano does: the harder you play, the brighter; a held chord rings on, the strings beating gently; with the sustain pedal (controller 64) the whole instrument resonates, and half pedalling works — a pedal partly down damps partly. The soft pedal (una corda, controller 67) shifts the hammers for the next notes: softer and darker.

The sustain pedal is also a knob — Sustain, on the PEDALS card in Play and Edit — so a song can pedal without a pedal: draw it as an automation lane (right-click the knob ▸ Create automation clip; points held, up on each chord change and down just after it, is how a pianist pedals), or let a modulator move it; half pedalling works there too. The pedal the strings hear is the higher of the knob and controller 64, so a real pedal still works on top of a lane. The demo With All Your Heart is pedalled this way.

  • Play shows the piano from above — every key's strings at their length over the spruce soundboard, the bridge, the cast frame's rim, the row of felt hammers along where they strike, the dampers — with the key you last played lit and its hammer raised, and the pedals: the sustain pedal's travel drawn over the law of how far the dampers are down at each position, with the Sustain, Dampers and Soft knobs under it. Below: the hammer (hardness, dynamics, velocity curve), the resonance (sympathy, decay, soundboard) and the output (tilt, width, gain).
  • Edit adds the tuning (A4, the stretch an aural tuner gives a piano's octaves, the strings' stiffness) with its curve in cents across the keyboard, the felt's stiffness, the strings' ring key by key, the unisons' spread, the bridge coupling and the phantom partials.
  • Ten presets (Concert Grand, Warm Ballad, Felt Piano, Honky-Tonk, Baroque Pitch at A = 415 Hz…) each come with four macros: hardness, resonance, body and decay.

Changing A4, the stretch or the stiffness designs the strings again, beside the window, once the knob has settled (a second or two; the editor says designing the strings with how far it has come): the piano keeps its previous tuning until the new one is ready. An export always waits for it. These three are set, not automated.

Plug-ins from other makers: installing and using them

Cantus plays instruments and effects made by others in the CLAP and VST3 formats, 64-bit. VST2 plug-ins (a lone .dll) and Apple's AU are not supported: when a plug-in offers several formats, install its CLAP or VST3 version.

On a Mac plug-ins live in /Library/Audio/Plug-Ins/CLAP and …/VST3 (for every user) or the same folders in your home's Library (for you); Cantus reads both. A plug-in there is a bundle (Name.clap, Name.vst3) and its editor opens in a window of its own that stays above Cantus while you work in it.

  1. Install it with its maker's installer, downloaded from the maker's own site. Leave it in the standard folder the installer proposes (C:\Program Files\Common Files\VST3 or …\CLAP).
  2. Let Cantus find it. Restart Cantus: at start it looks for new plug-ins by itself and says what it found. Without restarting, choose OPTIONS ▸ Scan for plug-ins (the top bar's menus, beside the logo), or press Scan for plug-ins on the picker's empty CLAP or VST3 shelf. The settings live in OPTIONS ▸ Plug-ins and folders… (Ctrl+, opens Settings; its Plug-ins page is in the sidebar; the picker's Find more plug-ins… goes there too). Later scans read only new and changed files. For a plug-in in another folder, first add the folder under Folders (Browse…, then Add) (Rescan everything reads every file again, blocked ones included). What it found is listed under Found; a file that crashed while being read is listed under Blocked, with Try again.
  3. An instrument: press the + above the track headers (a new instrument track), or click a track's instrument chip to swap its instrument. In the device picker, search by name or choose the CLAP or VST3 shelf, and press Enter.
  4. An effect: click an empty insert slot — in a mixer strip or in the inspector — and pick it the same way.
  5. Its own window, or Cantus's view. A plug-in opens its own editor when you insert or pick it. Cantus view shows its parameters instead, drawn as Cantus's own devices are: grouped by the plug-in's pages, a search field (by name or by page), the readings it reports (a gain reduction, a level) as meters. Switch between them with Plug-in window | Cantus view at the top of Cantus's view, under Editor in the insert's menu (right-click a slot, or its ⋯), or the Editor row of an instrument's inspector. As default (Use as default for this plug-in) makes the view showing the one this plug-in opens in from then on, in every project; click it again to go back to its own editor. The choice is yours, kept in your settings — not in the project — by the plug-in itself, so an update keeps it. A plug-in with no editor of its own has only Cantus's view. Switching never touches its sound, its state or its automation. Its values can be automated, modulated and mapped to a MIDI controller like those of Cantus's own devices, from either view.

Tonus: pitch correction for a voice

Tonus (in the picker's Pitch · vocal shelf) tunes a sung voice as it comes in — for the hard, stepped effect, for a natural correction, or for monitoring while you record (it adds 2.7 ms, reported to delay compensation).

  1. Put Tonus on the voice's track. Choose the key and the scale; on the keyboard, click a note to remove it (×) or bring it back (●), right-click to leave it alone (○).
  2. Speed: 0 ms (Hard) jumps from note to note; 50–100 ms corrects and keeps the voice's life. Amount sets how much is applied.
  3. To tune to what you play instead: MIDI notes (the notes held on a track, in any octave) or MIDI melody (the voice sings exactly the newest note) — choose the track under the switch; its clips and what you play on it live both count.
  4. More holds humanise (long notes keep their movement), flex (small deviations left alone), vibrato (the singer's, removed or doubled; or a created one), the formants and the throat (brighter or darker without changing the pitch), transposition and the reference A4.

The display shows what you sang (dim) and what comes out (bright), and the note you are singing with its distance in cents. Presets: Natural, Tight, Hard tune, Throat up, Throat down, MIDI lead and more. Details: docs/plugins/tonus.md.

Dynamics and analysis: Tonsor, Impetus, Discrimen, Speculum

Four effects shape a sound's peaks and attacks and show what is in it. Impetus starts on More punch and Discrimen on More attack; Tonsor, a safety, starts at 0 dBFS and changes nothing until a peak goes over, and Speculum only looks.

EffectWhat it doesWorth knowing
TonsorA clipper: cuts the peaks offCeiling, Drive, a Knee and three shoulders (Hard, Soft, Analog). Oversampling up to 16× keeps the harmonics it makes from folding back; True peak holds the ceiling between the samples too (about 1 ms of look-ahead), so a streaming service's −1 dBTP is really −1. Gain match takes the drive back out, so louder never wins. The meter shows how much (the deepest reduction), how often (the share of samples clipped in the last second), the overs between the samples in the input, and the output's true peak.
ImpetusMore or less attack and sustainWorks on level differences, not a threshold: a quiet hit and a loud one are shaped alike. Speed is how long an attack lasts, Length how long a tail counts as one. Highs or Lows shape only one side of a crossover (snap without the low end pumping, or a kick's thump); a sidechain lets another track's hits drive it. A hit through it draws what it does before you play.
DiscrimenSplits each hit into its transient and its bodyEach part has its own gain, a one-knob compressor and a tilt; Listen plays one part alone. Left alone the two add up to the input exactly. Zero latency costs nothing; Look-ahead (2 ms, compensated) catches every attack from its first sample.
SpeculumAn analyser that never touches the soundTabs: Overview, Spectrum (averaging, slope, peak hold, mid/side/left/right), Spectrogram, Scope (free, triggered, or on the beat), Vector (and the correlation: under 0 means trouble in mono), Loudness (momentary, short-term and integrated LUFS, range, true peak, against a target — the EBU R128 readings). ⋯ ▸ Dock in the workspace makes it a tab you can resize, split or tear out; dock several and watch them at once.

When a plug-in crashes

A plug-in made by someone else — a CLAP or VST3 effect or instrument — can crash or hang. Cantus runs those plug-ins in a helper process of their own (cantus-sandbox), so when one fails, the helper stops and Cantus keeps playing:

  • an effect that stopped passes the sound through untouched, in time with the rest of the mix (as if it were bypassed); an instrument that stopped falls silent;
  • a card in the bottom-right corner says so — "Glacier stopped working. Cantus kept playing without it." — with Reload and Keep it off, and what happened in a line of its own. Point anywhere on the card to read the whole account;
  • Reload loads the plug-in again with what you had set: its last good state and every value of the project. Several plug-ins that stopped together share one card and Reload all;
  • Keep it off leaves it stopped: its slot shows stopped in red (an instrument's track shows the sign on its icon and an outlined channel button; point at them for why), and its menu — in the inspector and on the mixer strip's insert line — offers Reload plug-in whenever you want it back. Asking for its window brings its card back;
  • a plug-in that crashes while it loads is told the same way: Reload it, or choose where it runs, and Cantus tries again. One that refuses to load, or does not load in time, is said as not loaded — never as one to install — and offers Reload plug-in in its menu; Cantus never tries it again by itself;
  • Cantus never waits for a plug-in. Loading one, its window, its state, moving it: each is asked of its helper, and the window goes on meanwhile. A slot whose plug-in is still loading passes the sound through, a track whose instrument is loading is silent, for the moment it takes;
  • the project loses nothing, and a save never pretends: saving asks every plug-in for its state as it is now and writes the file once they have answered. One that cannot answer in time is saved with the state it gave last, and the status line names it. Exports, bounces and freezes wait for their plug-ins the same way, and say so when one could not give its state as it was;
  • a plug-in that hangs is given two seconds, then its helper is ended; while it hangs, the audio never waits for it longer than a few milliseconds, and the window never waits at all. A plug-in showing a dialog of its own (a file to open, a licence) is not hung: Cantus waits for you;
  • a plug-in that keeps its helper waiting — a dialog of its own, perhaps behind Cantus — gets a card of its own — "Glacier is waiting for you", its whole name in the card's hint — with Show it (its dialog to the front) and Stop it (its helper is ended, Cantus plays on, and you can reload it; the card says when other plug-ins share that helper and would stop with it). What you ask of that helper meanwhile — another plug-in's window, a save — is done as soon as the dialog closes;
  • notes and knob moves sent while a plug-in was late are not lost: it gets them as soon as it answers.

Where a plug-in runs. Settings ▸ Plug-ins ▸ Where third-party plug-ins run sets the default for this computer: Together (one helper for all of them; the default), By maker (one per maker), Individually (a helper each: a crash stops only that plug-in) or Within Cantus (no helper: the fastest, and a crash closes Cantus). Each plug-in can differ: right-click its slot (or open Runs on a hosted instrument's row in the inspector) and choose. The choice is saved with the project; changing it moves the plug-in at once, with its sound. A plug-in that crashes three times in a session is offered Run individually on its card — by name, when the card lists the plug-ins it took down with it, which are reloaded where they were. Only the plug-in that crashed is counted. The plug-in manager lists the plug-ins that ever stopped on this computer (Forget clears one). Cantus's own effects and instruments always run within.

What it costs. A plug-in in a helper adds a round trip per audio block: a few microseconds while plug-ins follow one another, a few tens of microseconds after a pause (ADR 0063 has the measurements). Point at a hosted slot to see where it runs and its CPU; the status bar counts Plug-ins late each time a helper did not answer in time (the sound went on without the plug-in for a moment; a larger buffer gives it more time). Exports, stems, freezes and bounces wait for every block, so they are always exactly what the plug-in makes; if a plug-in stops working during one, Cantus says so instead of calling it done, and Stop ends it even while a plug-in hangs.

11. Projects, autosave and settings

  • File ▸ New project (Ctrl+N), Open… (Ctrl+O), Save (Ctrl+S), Save as… (Ctrl+Shift+S). A project is a .cantus file; saving keeps the previous one as .cantus.bak.
  • Audio files are collected when you save: recordings move into the project's Audio folder and imported files are copied there, so the folder travels whole. Collect audio and save does it on demand. A missing file shows MISSING on its clip; right-click it ▸ Locate file….
  • Autosave writes a recovery copy every minute when something changed (Settings ▸ Saving, 10 s to 10 min): <project>.cantus.autosave beside a saved project, or in %LOCALAPPDATA%\Cantus\autosave\ for an unsaved one. It never overwrites your file. Writing it never freezes the window (it runs in the background).
  • Recover your work: if Cantus closes unexpectedly — or you close it without saving — the next start offers what was left: the autosaved changes and any recorded takes that are in no saved file. Restore brings them back as one undo step (Ctrl+Z shows the last save); Discard deletes nothing. New and Open ask before replacing unsaved work.

Templates and the start page

File ▸ New from template… (or Start page in the command palette) opens the start page: at its left a card for each template, at its right your recent projects (the last twelve you opened or saved; one that is no longer where it was says so). Click a card for a new, untitled project made from it — Cantus asks first if the open one has unsaved changes, as New does; click a recent project to open it.

  • At start. The start page opens by itself when Cantus starts — after the audio setup of a first run and after a crash-recovery offer, and beside the start verse, never over it — unless Cantus was given a project to open (a file double-clicked, the command line) or restores work from a crash. Show at start, on the page itself or in Settings ▸ General, turns it off. Closing it (its ✕, Esc or a click outside) leaves the song Cantus opened on, as before.
  • Four starters come with Cantus, made from its own instruments and effects: Empty song (one instrument track), Beat (Pulsus II's kit, Gravis on the bass, an electric piano, a Reverb return, 92 BPM), Band recording (vocal, guitar, bass and keys audio tracks on inputs 1 and 2, a Reverb return, the metronome on) and Mastering (a track for your mix and the master's chain: equaliser, multiband compressor, stereo tool, maximiser and Speculum).
  • Your own: File ▸ Save as template… keeps the project as it is under a name (a template of the same name is replaced — the panel says so). It appears among the cards; hover it and click its × twice to delete it.
  • Track templates: File ▸ Save track as template… keeps the selected track — its instrument, colour, icon, level and pan, input and modulators — with its mixer strip: inserts, EQ, fader, sends and output (not its clips: a template is a sound, not a song). They appear on the Track templates shelf of the device picker and in the devices panel (drag one onto a track's header: it lands after that track) and in the command palette; the start page lists them under the recent projects, where a click on its × (twice) deletes one. Inserting one is one undo step: the track lands after the selected one, its strip takes the first blank mixer strip, and what it was routed to is found by name — a send to Reverb goes to this project's Reverb return; what this project does not have is left out, and the status line says what.

Templates live in Cantus's data folder (%LOCALAPPDATA%\Cantus\templates), project templates in projects, track templates in tracks; each is a .cantus file, so a template from an older version opens as any project does.

Settings (Ctrl+,, or the Options menu, which also opens each page directly; View ▸ Settings… still works). The window has a sidebar of pages, as FL's options: General (the verse at start, on or off, and the Spanish Bible), Audio (driver, devices, rate, buffer, latency, the record offset, and the Audio cores at work — the audio thread and the cores helping it, 1 for the audio thread alone — each count shown with an engine and a model it is known from, drawn at the right of the list and large under the field: 4 · V4 · Fulvia, 12 · V12 · Enzo, more than 16 a turbo-ramjet; the line under it says how many are at work now, counted the same way; the real-time priority each audio thread was given is the last line of the band), MIDI, Appearance (language — English or Español —, interface size, Units wear their own faces — on or off, see Each unit's face —, colours, keyboard shortcuts), Saving, Sample cache (its size limit — 16 GB by default; when full, the least recently used samples go first — and Clear cache; it lives in %LOCALAPPDATA%\Cantus\cache\samples, or in a portable copy's data folder), Updates, Plug-ins and AI (see AI agents below).

AI agents (MCP)

Cantus can be driven by an AI assistant you already use — Claude Code, Claude Desktop, Cursor, VS Code, Gemini CLI, LM Studio — through the Model Context Protocol (MCP). The assistant brings its model; Cantus brings the tools: tracks, notes, the mixer, effects and their parameters, automation, export. Every change an agent makes is one step in the undo history, named AI: … (AI: write the verse), and Ctrl+Z takes it back like any of yours. Cantus sends nothing to any AI service itself; what your assistant sends to its own is its business (read its privacy terms).

Turn it on. Settings ▸ AI ▸ Allow external agents: On. Cantus opens a door on this computer only — http://127.0.0.1:39571/mcp, the port changeable below it — protected by a token kept in your settings folder, readable by you alone. Copy the token puts it on the clipboard; Make a new token disconnects every agent (set each client up again).

Connect a client. Copy setup for has a button per client; each copies what that client wants, with its own token, and says where it goes:

  • Claude Code: a command — run it in a terminal (claude mcp add --transport http cantus …); claude mcp list then shows cantus connected while Cantus runs.
  • Claude Desktop: the lines for claude_desktop_config.json, which start the bridge cantus-mcp (installed beside Cantus); or Save the extension (.mcpb)… and open the file it saves.
  • Cursor, VS Code, Gemini CLI, LM Studio: the JSON for their MCP file (~/.cursor/mcp.json, .vscode/mcp.json, ~/.gemini/settings.json, LM Studio's mcp.json).

A client that starts a program instead of an address uses the bridge too; when Cantus is not running, or does not allow external agents, it answers with a message saying so.

What an agent may do. Reading the project and the transport (play, stop, locate) run at once. Edits ask you first: a card at the bottom right says what the agent wants to do — Claude Code wants to: …, what it would touch — with Allow, Allow for this session (that one tool, from that agent, until it disconnects) and Deny; with no answer within two minutes nothing is done. Edits: Without asking lets edits run at once. Removing or replacing your material, undoing and files always ask, whatever that setting says. Files are off until you choose Ask each time, and then reach only the project's folder and the folders you add below it. Never allow refuses a tool by its name. Names and notes inside a project are treated as data, never as instructions.

Watch and undo. While an agent is connected, a chip in the top bar shows its name (its light turns blue while one of its calls waits for you). A click opens the AI activity: the agents, each with Undo all of its changes — it undoes the agent's steps from the most recent and stops at the first one that is not the agent's, saying which — and Disconnect; the jobs at work (long renders), with their progress; and every call and what became of it, refusals included. A right-click on the chip opens Settings ▸ AI.

One switch. AI area: Off refuses every agent, closes the door and hides the area; Cantus is a complete DAW without it.

13. Privacy and updates

Cantus has no telemetry. An installed or portable copy asks GitHub once a day whether a newer version is out — an anonymous request that carries no version, machine or user information — and asks you before downloading anything; Settings ▸ Updates ▸ Check daily turns it off.

When you say yes, Cantus downloads the new version's file for your system, checks its SHA-256 against the release (and, on Windows, the publisher's signature when the build is signed) and applies it when you close Cantus — or now, with Restart and update:

  • Windows: the installer runs silently, keeping the components you chose.
  • Linux (AppImage): the new AppImage replaces the one you run, in the same place and under the same name. The old one is kept beside it, ending in .old, until the new one has started; if the new one ever fails to start, rename the .old back. In a folder Cantus may not write to, it says so and the release page has the file.
  • macOS: the new disk image opens when Cantus closes (Close and open the disk image does it now); drag Cantus onto Applications and choose Replace. (Replacing the app in place by itself is planned.)

A portable copy, a copy from a Linux distribution's packages or Flatpak, and a system no release has a file for are told about the new version and shown its page. Sound packs are downloaded only when you click Install. Everything else stays on your computer. The details are in PRIVACY.md; the licences of everything Cantus is built from are in THIRD-PARTY-NOTICES.md (also in Help ▸ About Cantus).

14. Known issues and limitations

This is a beta. What we know about:

  • Warping was measured, and tuned by ear on test material: tell us how a stretch sounds on your music. A plug-in that runs Together with many others costs a little more CPU than within Cantus (about 1.3× with 32 instances); choose Within Cantus for a plug-in you trust when the CPU is tight (§10, When a plug-in crashes).
  • A frozen track that loops plays, at the jump back to the loop's start, what led into that bar rather than the loop end's tail (a freeze is one straight pass, §6).
  • MP3 files cannot be imported (WAV, AIFF, FLAC and Ogg can).
  • No crossfades between separate clips (comp seams do crossfade, §4) and no clip gain envelopes.
  • Recorded automation writes every move made while a pass records (no latch or touch modes yet). In stems exported through the master chain, a hosted effect on the master is left out of the stems (the full mix has it).
  • Splitting a looping clip silences its right half: select the time range you need, copy it and paste it instead (the pasted part keeps the loop).
  • Automation clips are pasted only within the project they came from, while Cantus stays open; a first save or a Save As that copies imported audio into the project folder can pause the window while the files are copied; Ctrl+V with nothing copied does nothing and says nothing.
  • Chorda designs its strings for the audio device's sample rate when Cantus starts (a second or two, beside the window — the window never waits). Played before that, or at a sample rate it has not met (after changing the device), it is silent until the design is ready, and its editor says designing the strings with how far it has come. Its tuning parameters (A4, stretch, stiffness) are set, not automated or modulated: they offer no automation clip.
  • Lamina plays a little quieter than the other instruments (about 4 dB) at the same fader, and the Salamander Grand Piano about 5 dB quieter. DRSKit is a raw, uncompressed kit: a groove sits around −26 LUFS with peaks near −8 dBFS (at full strength it reaches −0.8 dBFS, near the most the headroom rule allows) — raise its fader or put a compressor on its strip, as with a recorded kit.
  • The Salamander's first load decodes its samples into the sample cache (1–2 GB on disk; about 10 s), beside the window; a note played before its samples arrive is silent. Later loads are quick.
  • Names in other scripts use your system's fonts (read when first needed, never shipped with Cantus). Arabic and Hebrew show left to right with unjoined letters, and scripts that join or reorder letters (Devanagari, Thai…) show them without that: the toolkit does not shape text yet.
  • Audio: the built-in Realtek ASIO driver delivers no sound on some laptops (use WASAPI or FlexASIO); ASIO4ALL can take several seconds to start; many ASIO drivers set the buffer in their own control panel; plug an interface in before starting Cantus. Exclusive-mode WASAPI is not offered.
  • Performance: dense projects at small buffers can drop out on modest computers; raise the buffer if the top bar counts dropouts.
  • The audio device cannot be changed while recording; the sample browser has no keyboard navigation yet; projects refer to sound-pack samples by their full path.
  • Not in this beta: the macOS and Linux installers (coming soon; the code builds and passes its tests on Linux), control-surface protocols (Mackie Control, HUI), several arrangements per project.
  • AI: an assistant panel inside Cantus (with your own key or a local model) and generating audio are not in this beta — agents connect from outside through MCP (§11). Stems and audio to MIDI run on the processor (the graphics processor is used only where the runtime offers one, on macOS); a separation of a three-minute song takes about a minute. A range an agent listens to starts a few seconds early when the project holds a hosted plug-in, and Space's modulation and Organum's rotary turn from their start, so such a range is close to, not identical with, the whole song (the answer says so).
