The stage: where a dancer dances

Back to the manual · Leer en español

See also: The dancer — its moves, moods and characters · Importing a character · Exporting a video — the song with its dancers (also from the companion's menu: Export video…).

A dancer is a track whose instrument makes no sound and draws a character that dances to your song. This page is about where it dances; its moves, moods and characters are the dancer's own window.

Over the workspace, on the Stage tab, or hidden

Each dancer has a place to show (its window's Where):

  • Over the workspace — the character stands right on top of your arrangement, mixer and editors, with nothing around it: no window, no frame, see-through. Its X and Y say where its feet are (0 to 1 across and down the workspace) and Size how tall it is (at 1×, about a third of the workspace's height). Automate X and it walks across the window.
  • On the Stage tab — the character dances on a stage of its own, a tab of the workspace that opens under the inspector the first time. Drag the tab anywhere, stack it beside others or float it as you would any other area; your layouts keep it.
  • Hidden — it shows nowhere and costs nothing.

Move it, resize it, pin it

Point at a dancer and its frame appears:

  • drag its body to move it (one undo step per drag: Move dancer);
  • drag the round grip at its top-right corner to make it bigger or smaller (its feet stay where they are);
  • in the pill above it: pin keeps it above the floating panels (a floating mixer, a device window); dock puts it on the Stage tab (on the tab, float puts it back over the workspace); hide hides it.

The frame fades a moment after the pointer leaves.

What it costs

A dancer is drawn only when its pose changes: at most 60 times a second while the song plays, 15 times a second while it is stopped (a slow breath and a blink), and not at all when it is hidden or Cantus is minimised. Stopped, once nothing has happened for ten seconds (no pointer over the window, no key, no Play), it holds its pose and costs nothing; the next thing you do wakes it where it was. While the companion window is open it keeps breathing, since that is what you stream. Each dancer is drawn into a box just big enough for it, at your screen's real pixels, so it is crisp at every zoom.

The companion window

View ▸ Companion window (Alt+Shift+D) opens a small window of its own, Cantus Stage, that floats above everything — Cantus, your browser, a game — with no frame and a see-through background. A character dances in it with whatever plays, with no automation: the song while it plays; a beat of its own at the project's tempo when the transport is stopped but sound comes out (a preview, your instrument through an input); a slow breath in silence. It dances bigger when the music is louder.

Drag it by anything in it to move it; drag its bottom-right corner to resize it. Point at it for its ⋯ button, or right-click anywhere in it, for its choices — each row steps to the next value when clicked:

  • Character — each of Cantus's characters, then The project's dancers (your dancer tracks, as they dance on the stage).
  • Size — three companion sizes, a square 600 × 600, a vertical 540 × 960 and a wide 960 × 540.
  • Background — transparent, the theme's stage, or a chroma green, blue or magenta.
  • Visualiser — the master's spectrum, its level, or off; Bar and beat and Song name and tempo on or off.
  • Always on top, Streaming mode, Copy OBS setup, and Close.

The menu also shows what it costs: the processor time of each of its frames, its frame rate and the share of one core they make. The graphics card's time is not measured (Cantus does not ask the driver), and the menu says so. Minimised, the window draws nothing and costs nothing. It stays open across sessions until you close it.

Streaming mode

View ▸ Streaming mode turns the companion into a capture source for OBS Studio or Streamlabs: the window keeps the same title, Cantus Stage, every session, shows nothing but the stage (no frame or buttons when you point at it — right-click still opens its menu), and its size and place are fixed and remembered. Copy OBS setup in its menu puts the steps for its background on the clipboard.

On Windows, OBS's Game Capture could not capture the window on a laptop with two graphics chips (one draws Cantus, the other runs OBS); Window Capture always did. On Linux, use Window Capture (Xcomposite) or Screen Capture (PipeWire); a desktop with a compositor keeps the transparency, and without one choose a chroma background.
